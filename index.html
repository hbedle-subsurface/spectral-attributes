What you see in seismic depends on the frequencies you look at.

A bed that is invisible at one frequency is obvious at another, so a 30 Hz image and a 60 Hz image of the same survey can show different geology — without either one being wrong. Channels, thin sands and fault-related thinning all show up this way.

Most interpreters arrive knowing the conclusions and not the machinery, which is fine until a parameter has to be chosen, two volumes disagree, or a map turns out to be showing the acquisition. Here you can move the window and see the resolution trade, thin a bed and watch the notch march up the spectrum, whiten a spectrum and watch the feature you wanted go with it.

Everything is computed live in your browser from the numbers on screen. Nothing is a stored picture, so the panels can and sometimes do disagree with the textbook version of the same figure.

What you need first

An introductory geophysics course, or the equivalent: that reflections come from contrasts in acoustic impedance, that a trace is reflectivity convolved with a wavelet, and that seismic times are two-way. Module 00 restates all three. No mathematics beyond a sine wave, and nothing to calculate by hand.

What you can do afterwards

Read a spectrum and say what its bandwidth, peak frequency and Nyquist limit mean for the data in front of you. Explain why a channel looks different at 20 Hz and at 50 Hz. Estimate a bed thickness from a peak frequency and state the range over which that estimate holds. Read an RGB blend and name the three choices behind it.

If you only have two hours

Modules 00, 02, 04 and 06 make a coherent short course: why frequency matters, the resolution trade that limits every method, tuning and the thin bed, and how a spectrum everywhere becomes a map. Module 09 is the natural fifth if the topic is interpretation rather than method.

What else AASPI does

These modules cover the spectral tools an interpreter meets first. The AASPI package contains several more, left out here because they are hard to use well before the basics are secure. They are listed so that a name met on a menu is at least familiar.

  • Spectral slope and roughness. Fitted to the log-log spectrum at every sample. A sharp interface gives a flat spectrum, a thin bed one that rises with frequency, and an upward-fining or upward-coarsening ramp one that falls — so the slope discriminates between kinds of contact rather than measuring thickness. Roughness records how badly that straight-line model fits.
  • Spectral kurtosis and skewness. Statistical measures of the shape of the spectrum, used where the balancing in module 05 has made the usual Gaussian assumptions inappropriate, and sometimes used to estimate attenuation between two picked horizons.
  • Phase residues. Points where the spectral phase fails to close around a small loop, computed after the traveltime correction in module 03. They mark places where the phase field is singular, which often coincides with thin-bed edges and faults.
  • Phase decomposition. Separating a volume into components at −90°, 0°, +90° and 180°, on the argument that a thin bed and an interface have different phase signatures.
  • Bandwidth extension (spec_cwt and thin_bed_decomposition). Two different algorithms, one following wavelet transform ridges and one fitting reflection doublets, both recovering frequencies outside the recorded band by assuming the earth is sparse. Module 08's warning about model-based methods applies with full force.
  • Variational mode decomposition (spec_vmd). A different way of splitting a trace into components, not based on a transform at all.
  • Spectral probe (spectral_probe). A normalized crosscorrelation with a cosine exactly one period long, which behaves like a spectral voice with a short automatic gain control. Short probes have been shown to image strike-slip faults better than the broadband data.
  • Two-dimensional wavelet transform (kxky_cwt). The same idea applied to spatial wavenumbers on a time slice rather than to frequency down a trace, used to suppress acquisition footprint.

The AASPI documentation is the reference for all of these. It is also, in places, inconsistent with itself — the prewhitening constant appears as three different numbers, and two program names are used for what appears to be one program. Where these modules met such a case they say so rather than choosing silently.

About these modules

Every panel is generated from the parameters on screen. There are no stored images and no curves drawn to look plausible, so the models can be pushed until they break, which is usually where the lesson is. Each module carries a Method section stating what is left out and where the browser version departs from production software.

Everything runs in the browser. No installation and no account. Nothing you do inside a module — no slider, no click, no trace you generate — is transmitted anywhere. The stylesheet, the three typefaces and every line of module code are served from this site, so opening a page contacts no other domain. The one thing that leaves the browser is a page count: each page loads a small counting script from gc.zgo.at, which records the page address, the page title, the address you arrived from, your screen size and the browser string your browser sends to every site it visits. No cookie is set and no identifier is stored, so one visit cannot be linked to the next. We keep that count so the modules people actually use are the ones that get improved; assets/count.js says exactly what is sent and how to switch it off.
