Module 04  ·  Spectral attributes

Tuning, and what a thin bed does to amplitude

Module 03 ended with a wedge that was brightest in a different place at every frequency, and a readout that kept tracking 1/(2f). This is where that relation comes from. A bed thinner than the wavelet returns one composite event rather than two reflections, and its amplitude, its apparent thickness and its spectrum all change in ways that can be read backwards into a thickness — over a limited range, which these five steps measure.

1Amplitude and apparent thickness down a wedge

Two curves that every interpreter should know by heart

Why this step is here. A sand thins across a field. At what thickness do its top and base stop being two separate reflections, and what does the seismic show after that?

The two curves below are measured off that section, trace by trace. The upper one is the peak amplitude of the composite event. The lower one is the apparent thickness: the time between the peak and the trough, which is what picking both events and subtracting would give.

Why the lower curve decides more than the upper one. A net-pay map built from a seismic isochron is built from the lower curve. Above the tuning thickness that curve is the true thickness and the map is right. Below it the curve flattens: every bed thinner than tuning is reported at very nearly the same thickness, so the map returns one value across the whole thin part of the field. A volume computed from it is too large, and it is too large by an amount that grows as the bed thins, which places the worst error exactly where the reservoir is most marginal and the decision hardest.

The upper curve is what is left to work with. Below tuning the amplitude keeps falling as the bed thins, so amplitude rather than picked thickness carries what remains of the thickness information. Turning an amplitude into a thickness requires the impedance contrast to be known independently, from a well or from an inversion, which is why below-tuning thickness work is calibrated rather than measured.

The frequency and the noise decide how far the bed can be followed. On a gas sand with no noise, the bed stays above the bedding around it down to 4.5 ms of thickness at 18 Hz, 1.5 ms at 30 Hz and 1.1 ms at 45 Hz. Holding the wavelet at 30 Hz and adding noise moves the limit the other way: 1.5 ms at 10% noise, 3.4 ms at 30% and 7.9 ms at 60%. The impedance contrast decides whether a bed is visible at all, before thickness enters into it. Measured against the background bedding on this section, a coal in sandstone reflects at 5.32 times bedding strength and a limestone in shale at 4.56, so both stand out. A gas sand in shale gives 2.81. A sand in shale gives 0.36, and a wet sand in shale 0.05, both weaker than the bedding around them and not separable on the section at any thickness. The reflection coefficients behind those figures are −0.393, +0.337, −0.207, +0.026 and +0.004, computed from the velocities and densities in the seismic resolution modules. Tuning describes how a visible bed behaves as it thins; it does not make an invisible bed appear.

Below the tuning thickness the apparent thickness stops responding. With a 30 Hz wavelet the amplitude peaks at 13.0 ms and the apparent thickness bottoms out at 11.1 ms. Above that the two agree well — 44.9 ms picked against 44.9 ms true. Below it the pick stops falling: where the bed is really 2.0 ms thick the pick still reads 11.2 ms, five and a half times too thick, and there is nothing on the section to say so. The picks are clean, the horizons are pickable, and the number is fiction. The amplitude curve has its own ambiguity: it rises to a maximum and falls away again, so a bright composite can mean a bed at tuning or a thicker bed with a stronger contrast. Neither curve recovers the thickness on its own. The spectrum can, over a range, which is the next two steps.

2Where the notch comes from

Two reflections, arithmetic, one gap in the spectrum

Why this step is here. The spectrum of a trace over a thin bed has holes in it at regular intervals. Is that noise, a processing artifact, or the bed?

The mechanism is one frequency at a time. The base reflection is delayed by T relative to the top and carries the opposite polarity. Those two facts together decide whether the pair adds or cancels at each frequency.

Where the delay is a whole number of cycles the two arrivals line up crest on crest, and the polarity flip then puts them exactly out of phase, so they cancel. Where the delay is half a cycle the polarity flip brings them into phase and they add. The spectrum therefore alternates between cancellation and reinforcement at a spacing set by T alone.

The result is the comb below: notches spaced 1/T apart starting at zero frequency, with the first peak at 1/(2T), halfway between the first two notches. The comb goes on repeating above that, and every one of its peaks is the same height — 3/(2T), 5/(2T) and so on. Only the wavelet decides which of them is the tallest on the measured spectrum, which is where module 09 picks the story up.

The comb belongs to the bed; the envelope belongs to the wavelet. The gray curve is what a pair of spikes T apart would give on their own — pure interference, no wavelet involved. The teal curve is the same comb seen through the wavelet's own spectrum, which is why the notches are only visible where the wavelet had energy to remove. Push the wavelet's peak frequency down and the higher notches disappear, not because the bed changed but because there was nothing there to notch. That is the practical limit that matters most here: a notch can only be measured if the recorded bandwidth reaches it. The measured notch tracks the prediction closely while it is inside the band — 16.5 Hz against a predicted 18.2 on a 55 ms bed, 21.5 against 22.3 at 45 ms, 28.5 against 28.7 at 35 ms — and then, for beds thinner than about 25 ms, the readout says "out of band" rather than guessing, because with a 30 Hz wavelet there is no energy left at 40 Hz for a notch to show up in.

3Reading thickness back out of a spectrum

Two independent estimates, from the same curve

Why this step is here. If the notch positions are set by the bed thickness, can the bed thickness be read back out of them?

The panel below tracks both estimates against the truth, all the way down the wedge. Neither works everywhere, and the two ranges over which they do work barely overlap.

Which method suits which target follows from the panel. Peak frequency sits at 1/(2T), so it stays inside the recorded band for beds near and somewhat above the tuning thickness: a channel fill, a single amalgamated sand body, a thin carbonate. Notch spacing is 1/T and needs at least two notches inside the band, which requires a thicker interval: a stacked turbidite lobe, a coal-bearing package with well-defined bounding surfaces, a shale-encased sand tens of milliseconds thick. Between the two ranges lies a band of thicknesses that neither method addresses, and a thickness quoted from within it has been extrapolated rather than measured.

Each estimate has a working range, and the two ranges barely overlap. The notch estimate is good on thick beds — 52.6 ms read against 49.9 true, 35.1 against 34.8 — and gives up below about 25 ms, where the notch has climbed out of the top of the band. Peak frequency is the opposite: good between roughly 13 and 28 ms with this wavelet, and failing at both ends, because too thick puts 1/(2T) below the wavelet's spectrum and too thin puts it above. Either way the answer stops moving: on a thin bed the measured peak reverts to the wavelet's own peak, and on a thick bed the next comb peak up, at 3/(2T), has moved into the band and is read instead of the first one. Module 09 separates those two cases; here they fail the same way. Notice what that means: the range is a property of the wavelet, not of the earth. A 20 Hz wavelet gives 19.2 to 42.9 ms; a 60 Hz wavelet gives 6.6 to 13.1 ms. Both are about one octave of thickness wide — the range slides with the bandwidth but never gets much wider. Which is exactly why the next module is about flattening the wavelet out of the way before any of this is attempted.

4Two definitions of the tuning thickness

And they are different numbers

Why this step is here. Two sources give the tuning thickness of the same 30 Hz wavelet as 13 ms and as 17 ms. Which one is wrong?

  • Amplitude tuning. The thickness at which the composite reflection is brightest — the peak of the upper curve in step 1. This is what most people mean when they say a bed is "at tuning."
  • Spectral tuning. 1/(2f), the thickness implied by the peak of the spectrum. This is what module 03's readout was tracking and what every spectral thickness estimate uses.

Both are measured below as the wavelet's peak frequency changes. The gap between them is fixed.

1/(2f) in two-way time is the quarter wavelength. Introductory courses give the tuning thickness as λ/4, and this page has been giving it as 1/(2f), without either statement mentioning the other. They are the same number. A wavelength is v/f. A quarter of it is v/(4f), which is a length in the ground. Seismic measures two-way time, so a bed v/(4f) thick delays the second reflection by 2 × v/(4f) ÷ v = 1/(2f). The velocity cancels, which is why the seismic version carries no velocity in it and the textbook version does. The readouts above express both tuning thicknesses as fractions of a wavelength, and the spectral one sits at a quarter whatever the frequency and whatever the velocity.
They differ by a fixed ratio, and neither is wrong. They are answers to two different questions. Amplitude tuning asks where constructive interference between two wavelets is strongest, which depends on the shape of the wavelet. Spectral tuning asks where the interference comb peaks, which depends only on the bed. The second is independent of the wavelet, which is why it is the one used for thickness estimation. It is also why two people can quote different tuning thicknesses for the same bed, each convinced the other has made an arithmetic error. The measured ratio is 0.780, and it holds there across the whole frequency range, because both quantities scale as one over the wavelet frequency and their ratio depends only on the wavelet's shape. So the disagreement is 22%, systematically, in the same direction every time — worse than a random error, because it never averages out. A bed described as being at tuning is ambiguous until it is said which tuning.

5The floor, and what noise does to it

Where the measurement stops being a measurement

Why this step is here. A bed keeps thinning below the tuning thickness. What stops the seismic from following it further?

The two traces below are a bed of the chosen thickness and one half as thick, each scaled to the same peak. Taking the thickness down brings the two waveforms together, and the readouts measure how far apart they still are.

Where a stratigraphic question stops being answerable. Once two thicknesses return the same waveform to within the noise, a single reflection is consistent with a bed of either. Whole classes of unit sit permanently in that range: a coal seam, a transgressive lag, a crevasse splay, the individual beds of a heterolithic tidal package. They are visible, often strikingly so, because a large impedance contrast at small thickness still returns a strong reflection. Their thickness is not measurable. Detection and measurement part company here, and reading a strong event as a thick one is the specific error this floor produces.

The shape and the spectrum give up together, and that is the real limit. At 24 ms against 12 ms the two waveforms differ by 126% and the peak frequencies are 24.9 and 33.2 Hz — easily told apart by either route. Halve everything and the difference halves too: 78.5%, then 42.4%, then 21.6% at 3 ms against 1.5 ms, where the peak frequencies have converged to 36.7 and 36.8 Hz. A tenth of a hertz apart is not a measurement. Below this point the composite is simply the derivative of the wavelet with a scale factor on it, and that scale factor is thickness times impedance contrast — one number where there were two. A thin strong bed and a thinner stronger bed are the same trace, and nothing recovers them separately. This is Widess's result, and no processing has ever gotten around it.

Why it matters

Where this shows up in real work

This module is what turns spectral decomposition from a picture into a measurement, and marks the range over which the measurement holds.

  • Mapping a reservoir too thin to pick. A bed below the picking floor still puts its thickness into the spectrum, and a thickness map made that way is a real deliverable — provided the target sits inside the working range, which is the first thing to check rather than the last.
  • Not over-reading an amplitude map. A bright spot can be a thicker bed, a stronger contrast, or a bed sitting at tuning. Step 1's amplitude curve is why those cannot be separated from amplitude alone.
  • Knowing when a picked isochron is a floor rather than a measurement. Below tuning, the pick separation stops responding, and a thickness map made from picks flattens out at a value that has nothing to do with the rocks.
  • Judging whether a survey can answer the question at all. If 1/(2T) for the target lies above the recorded bandwidth, no processing recovers it — that is an acquisition answer, not a processing one.
  • Reading someone else's tuning number. Two definitions are in use, and the ratio between them is not small.

The errors with the largest consequences here. The most damaging is treating a thickness derived from peak frequency as though it had the accuracy of a well tie. It has a working range about one octave of thickness wide, and outside that range it returns a number rather than a warning. The second is quoting a tuning thickness without saying which of the two definitions it is, since the two differ by a fixed ratio and a reader who assumes the other one is out by that much.

The seismic resolution modules approach the same limit from the other side, asking what can be separated rather than what can be measured. Their tuning thickness is the amplitude tuning thickness defined in step 4 here: they measure it at about 1/(2.6f), which is 12.8 ms for a 30 Hz wavelet against the 13.0 ms measured on this page. The two sets are consistent, and Kallweit and Wood (1982) is the source of that coefficient in both.

Exercises

  1. Find the floor in the apparent thickness. Why: a picked isochron below this value is reporting the wavelet rather than the bed. In step 1, walk the trace slider down the wedge from thick to thin, reading the true and apparent thickness at each stop. Before starting: as the bed thins toward its pinch-out, does the picked thickness follow it to zero, stop somewhere, or something else?
    HintThey track each other closely down to about the tuning thickness — 44.9 ms against 44.9 at trace 30 — and then the apparent value stops falling and settles on a floor of 11.1 ms. At trace 110, where the bed is really 4.5 ms, the pick reads 11.3 ms. Below the floor every bed reads the same thickness no matter how thin it is. This is why a thickness map made by picking a top and a base goes flat over the thin part of a reservoir and gives no indication that anything has gone wrong — the picks are still perfectly pickable, and the number they produce is meaningless. Raise the wavelet's peak frequency and the floor drops, because the floor is a property of the wavelet rather than of the rocks.
  2. Watch a notch move. Why: the notch spacing is set by the bed thickness alone, which is what makes it measurable. In step 2, take the trace slider across the wedge and watch the first notch.
    HintThe notch sits at 1/T, so it moves up in frequency as the bed thins — slowly over the thick part of the wedge, then faster and faster. The measured and predicted values agree closely while the notch is inside the wavelet's bandwidth, and the measurement fails once it climbs out the top. Now drop the wavelet's peak frequency to 15 Hz and watch how much of the wedge loses its notch entirely. The bed has not changed at all. The bandwidth available to interrogate it has.
  3. Find each estimate's working range. Why: each estimator returns a number everywhere and is right over about one octave of thickness. In step 3, read the working-range readout, then change the wavelet's peak frequency and read it again.
    HintWith a 30 Hz wavelet the peak-frequency estimate is good between 13.1 and 27.7 ms. At 20 Hz that becomes 19.2 to 42.9 ms, at 45 Hz it is 8.6 to 18.2, and at 60 Hz it is 6.6 to 13.1. Two consequences. First, the range slides with the wavelet but stays about one octave of thickness wide, so there is never a wide range, only a differently placed one. Second, and more useful: the range is set entirely by the wavelet, so it is a fact about the data rather than about the target. A reservoir outside that range is not reached by any amount of care with the decomposition. Meanwhile the notch estimate covers the thick end, where peak frequency fails. Neither is general; together they cover more than either alone.
  4. Measure the gap between the two tuning definitions. Why: a tuning thickness quoted without its definition is ambiguous by a fixed ratio. In step 4, take the wavelet peak frequency across its range and read the ratio.
    HintThe ratio stays essentially constant while both numbers change by a factor of several — because both scale as one over the wavelet frequency, so their ratio is a property of the wavelet's shape rather than of its frequency. For a Ricker it is 0.780, and the readout holds at 0.780 from 15 Hz to 60 Hz. The practical consequence is that quoting "the" tuning thickness without naming the definition introduces an error of the same size as the ratio, systematically and in the same direction every time, which is worse than a random error because it never averages out.
  5. Find three explanations for one bright amplitude. Why: a bright amplitude has several causes, and naming one without testing the others is the commonest error in this module. In step 1, note the amplitude at the tuning thickness. Then think about what else could produce that same brightness on a real map, and use the sliders to produce each one.
    HintThere are at least three. A bed sitting at the tuning thickness is brightest by construction. A thicker bed with a larger impedance contrast reaches the same amplitude because the reflection coefficients are larger — the model here holds them at ±0.1, but on real data they vary with lithology and fluid. And a bed thinner than tuning with a larger contrast does the same. Raise the wavelet's peak frequency and the thickness that is brightest moves, without any bed changing at all, which is a fourth. The amplitude of a composite reflection is a product of thickness and contrast, and a single number cannot separate a product into its two factors. That is why an amplitude map alone cannot be read as a thickness map, and how the spectral estimate in step 3 differs: it responds to thickness and not to contrast.
  6. Fill a notch with noise. Why: a notch is an absence of energy, so added noise fills a real one in or leaves a gap where there is none, and the estimate comes back confidently wrong. Put the trace slider on a 25 ms bed with step 3 open, then raise the noise slider from zero to 60%.
    HintThe peak-frequency estimate barely moves: 20.4 ms at no noise, 21.8 ms at 60%. The notch estimate does something worse than degrade — at 30% noise it reports 153.8 ms for a 24.7 ms bed. It has not become vague, it has found a dip in the noise and called it a notch, and it reports that with exactly the same confidence as a correct answer. This is the difference between an estimator that depends on where energy is and one that depends on where energy is not: noise is energy, so it can only remove a real notch or manufacture a false one. It is the practical reason production thickness estimation leans on peak frequency, even though notch spacing is the cleaner idea and the easier one to explain.

Key points

  • A thin bed puts a comb in the spectrum. Notches at 1/T, 2/T, 3/T and a maximum at 1/(2T), produced by interference between the top and base reflections and by nothing else.
  • That comb is a thickness measurement. Read the peak frequency and invert it, or measure the notch spacing, and the bed's thickness falls out — with no need to separate the two reflections.
  • Each estimator has a working range about one octave of thickness wide, set by the wavelet rather than by the earth. Peak frequency covers the middle, notch spacing covers thick beds, and outside their ranges both report the wavelet's own tuning instead.
  • Amplitude tuning and spectral tuning are different numbers. For a Ricker wavelet the ratio is 0.780 and holds at every frequency, but it is a property of the wavelet's shape, so a different wavelet gives a different ratio. Both are called the tuning thickness. Always ask which one is meant.
  • Below tuning, picked thickness stops responding and settles on a floor set by the wavelet, with no indication on the section that anything is wrong.
  • Noise fills real notches and creates false ones. A notch is an absence of energy, so added noise either raises the floor inside a real one or leaves a gap where there is none, and the answer comes back confident and wrong. Peak frequency is less sensitive to noise, because it depends on where the energy is rather than on where it is missing.

Words introduced here

wedge model
A bed of constant properties thinning steadily to a pinch-out. The standard test object for this subject.
tuning
The interference between the top and base reflections of a bed once they are close enough in time to overlap.
apparent thickness
The time between the peak and the trough of the composite event. What picking both events would give, which stops responding below the tuning thickness.
amplitude tuning thickness
The bed thickness at which the composite amplitude is greatest. About 1/(2.6f), or 13.0 ms for a 30 Hz Ricker. This is the quantity the seismic resolution modules call the tuning thickness.
quarter wavelength
The usual textbook statement of the tuning thickness. A quarter of v/f in the ground, which is 1/(2f) in two-way time, so the two statements are the same number and the velocity cancels out of the seismic one.
spectral tuning thickness
1/(2f), the thickness whose spectral peak sits at f. 16.7 ms at 30 Hz. Also called the tuning thickness, and a different number.
notch
A frequency at which the top and base reflections cancel. Notches sit at 1/T, 2/T, 3/T for a bed of two-way thickness T.
comb
The regular pattern of notches and peaks a thin bed imposes on the spectrum.
Widess limit
The thickness below which the composite waveform stops changing shape and only gets quieter.

Method

What is computed, and what is left out

The wedge is 120 traces, each a reflection coefficient of +0.1 at a fixed top and −0.1 at a base that steps down linearly to the pinch-out, convolved analytically with a zero-phase Ricker. The reflection times are not rounded to the sample grid, so the tuning curves come out smooth rather than stair-stepped — an early version that rounded them produced a visibly terraced amplitude curve that looked like a real effect.

The measured quantities in step 1 are read off each trace rather than derived. Amplitude is the largest absolute value in the wedge interval. Apparent thickness is the time between the maximum and the following minimum, located to sub-sample precision by fitting a parabola through the three samples around each extremum, which is what stops the curve from stair-stepping.

The spectra are direct discrete Fourier transforms of the trace over a fixed window around the wedge, with a Hann taper. The comb drawn in gray behind them is 2|sin(πfT)|, the closed-form spectrum of two opposite-polarity spikes T apart, scaled to the same peak. The first notch is located by walking up the spectrum to the first local minimum below a fraction of the running peak, and interpolating.

How the peak frequency is picked here, and why it differs from the rest of the set. This page takes the tallest maximum of the spectrum wherever it falls. Modules 05 through 09 instead take the lowest-frequency maximum reaching 70% of the tallest, which is the one that inverts to the correct thickness and is what production software approximates with a frequency search range. The unguarded version is used here on purpose: it is what lets step 3 show the estimate leaving its working range at both ends and reporting a confident wrong number, which a guarded picker would hide.

Simplifications, stated plainly. The bed is a clean doublet: two isolated reflection coefficients, equal and opposite, in a constant background, with nothing above or below to interfere. Real intervals have internal layering, gradational contacts, and neighbours close enough to add their own interference, all of which put structure in the spectrum that this model cannot produce. The wavelet is zero phase and stationary, so nothing here shows what a phase rotation or attenuation does to a notch — module 09 returns to the second of those. There is no spectral balancing applied anywhere in this module, which is deliberate: it lets module 05 show what difference balancing makes by starting from exactly this state. And the noise is band-limited and added after convolution, which is the friendliest form noise takes.

Where this departs from production software. Production thickness estimation does not measure a notch on a single trace. It computes peak frequency at every sample of a balanced volume, usually from a wavelet transform or matching pursuit rather than a windowed Fourier transform, and maps 1/(2f) over an interval. The single-trace picture here is the mechanism behind that, not the workflow. AASPI's thin_bed_decomposition takes a different route again, fitting doublets directly to the trace rather than inferring them from a spectrum.
