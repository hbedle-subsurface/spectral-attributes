Module 09  ·  Spectral attributes

Three ways to make a low-frequency anomaly, and one to miss a reservoir

A low-frequency anomaly on a spectral attribute map is where this work becomes useful and where most of the errors are made. Four different things produce one, and their spectra at the target are not distinguishable. These five steps build all four, show what separates them, and end with a set of tests that an interpreter can actually run.

2What attenuation does to a wavelet

A wavelet, propagated through absorbing rock

Why this step is here. Absorbing rock is said to lower the frequency of what passes through it. By how much, and does it look like a thin bed?

The quantity that describes how lossy a rock is is Q, the quality factor. High Q means low loss: a stiff, well-cemented, low-porosity rock might reach several hundred. Unconsolidated sands and shales, and rocks with mobile pore fluid, run much lower. Low Q over gas is widely reported, but the mechanism and the size of the effect are still argued over, so treat a low-Q zone as evidence worth following rather than as a fluid indicator on its own.

The amplitude at frequency f, after travelling for time t, is multiplied by exp(−πft/Q). Because that factor depends on f, it changes the shape of the spectrum as well as its size, and the shape change is what moves the peak down.

Attenuation produces exactly the signature a thin bed produces. A 32.2 Hz wavelet propagated through 600 ms of rock comes back at 29.9 Hz for Q = 200, at 26.8 Hz for Q = 80, at 20.2 Hz for Q = 30 and at 11.1 Hz for Q = 10. Fed through 1/(2f), those become thicknesses too large by 1.2, 3.1, 9.2 and 29.4 ms, with no bed involved anywhere. At Q = 30 the loss at 60 Hz is 98% — the high frequencies have not been cancelled by interference, they have been absorbed, and nothing about the spectrum at the target says which of the two happened.

3The low-frequency shadow

The clue is below the reservoir, not at it

Why this step is here. If the spectra at the target are identical, what else is different?

Two effects stack here and are worth separating. Passing down through the gas sand and back up through it removes high frequencies twice, which is a fixed penalty applied to everything below. On top of that, the section beneath the sand is itself low-Q in this model, so the loss keeps building with depth. The first produces the step at the reservoir, the second the downward trend below it. Both are present on real data, and the trend is usually the larger.

The section below is what this looks like on seismic. A gas sand sits at 150 ms, covering part of the line, with identical stratigraphy everywhere else. The traces that pass through the sand carry the loss; the traces either side of it do not. Widening or narrowing the body moves the edges of the shadow with it.

Why the shadow is the useful observation and the anomaly is not. The spectrum at the target is the same for all four causes, so nothing measured there separates them. The shadow is a statement about the interval the wave passed through, and only one of the four causes involves passing through anything absorbing. It is also evidence about geometry: the shadow is as wide as the body above it, so its edges mark the lateral extent of whatever absorbed, and that outline can be compared with the outline mapped from amplitude. Where the two agree, the same body produced both. Where the shadow is wider than the amplitude anomaly, the amplitude has been limited by tuning rather than by the extent of the body.

The shadow has the same edges as the body that caused it. On clean data at Q = 30 the measurement level reads about 35 Hz off the body and 11.8 Hz under it, a drop of 23.5 Hz, which is 5.7 times the trace-to-trace scatter of the measurement made off the body. Comparing against that scatter is the test, rather than against a fixed number of hertz. Taking the body to zero width removes the shadow, which is the control worth running.
Noise takes the shadow zone before it takes the rest of the section. Raising the noise to 10% does not weaken the shadow; it reverses it. The measurement under the body reads about 12 Hz higher than off it. A shadow zone is dim by definition — the energy that made it low-frequency is energy it lost — so noise dominates there while the brighter section either side is still readable, and the peak frequency of noise is not low. On a real section this appears as a low-frequency anomaly that vanishes or inverts when the data are reprocessed with a different noise attenuation, and that behavior is itself the diagnostic.

Below the gas sand the frequency stays down; below the tuned bed it comes back. At Q = 30 the line reads 28.3 Hz above the absorbing body, 25.8 Hz at the target, and 26.0 Hz two hundred milliseconds below it — still well under the 28.3 Hz measured above. Take Q to 15 and the reading two hundred milliseconds below collapses to 5.0 Hz, while the value above the body barely moves at 27.7 Hz. Beneath the tuned bed the frequency recovers by 4.1 Hz, back toward the background. This is the low-frequency shadow: a shadow cast downward by something the energy passed through. It has two uses. A shadow is evidence about the body above it, so an anomaly with a shadow beneath it indicates that something absorbed. And an anomaly with no shadow beneath it cannot be attenuation, which removes one of the four causes from one further look at data that is already available.

4Tuning lobes and the wavelet

A thicker bed, and a processing choice

Why this step is here. Two more causes produce the same anomaly and are less often named. What are they?

The panels show both. On the left, bed thickness is plotted against the peak frequency it produces, so a vertical line at one frequency shows every thickness that could have caused it. On the right, identical geology under two different wavelets. Frequency runs left to right on both, as it does on every spectrum in the set.

What the left panel means for a prospect. One peak frequency is produced by more than one thickness, because the interference peaks repeat at 1/(2T), 3/(2T), 5/(2T) and so on. A low peak frequency read as a thick gas-charged sand is equally consistent with a much thinner bed sitting on a higher lobe. Stratigraphic context is what settles it: a channel form of known scale, a well tie a few kilometers away, or the thickness the depositional system is capable of producing. The seismic alone does not.

The right panel carries a different warning. Identical rock under two wavelets produces two spectra, so a spectral anomaly present in a reprocessed volume and absent in the original is a statement about the processing sequence. This is the cause most often missed, because a reprocessing is usually undertaken to improve something and the improvement is what gets examined, rather than the attributes computed afterward.

Both are ambiguities rather than errors. A bed tunes at 1/(2T), again at 3/(2T) and again at 5/(2T), so as it thickens the measured peak follows 1/(2T) down until the next lobe carries more energy and the reading jumps. Which lobe is measured depends on the wavelet, so the same bed under two different sources reports two different frequencies. Neither measurement is wrong; the map simply does not record which lobe it read.

5Four tests that separate the causes

Four tests, run on all four anomalies

Why this step is here. If no single spectrum identifies the cause, what does?

  • Is there a shadow? Compare the peak frequency well below the target with the background. Only attenuation casts one.
  • Does the magnitude drop too? Attenuation removes energy; tuning redistributes it.
  • Does it move when the wavelet changes? A processing artifact tracks the wavelet; geology does not.
  • Is the notch spacing consistent? A genuine thin bed has notches at 1/T, 2/T, 3/T; a bed on a higher lobe has them closer together than its peak suggests.

None of the four is a spectral measurement at the target, and that is the point of the module. Three of them ask what is above, below or beside the anomaly, and the fourth asks what happens when the processing changes. A cause is established by the pattern across the four rather than by any one, which is the same standard applied to a direct hydrocarbon indicator: how many independent observations agree, not how strong the strongest one is.

The failure this guards against is specific. Four causes produce the same anomaly, three of them are ordinary and one is a reservoir, so an anomaly taken at face value is one candidate out of four rather than a finding. The four tests run on data that are already on disk, and the outcome is either a shorter list of causes or an anomaly that has survived all four, which is a different thing from an anomaly that was never questioned.

The four tests together separate all four causes, and no single test does. Attenuation changes the frequency 3.2 Hz below the target and the others do not, which is the shadow test. Tuning drops the magnitude with the frequency and attenuation does not. The lobe ambiguity moves when the wavelet changes and nothing else does. A processing difference shows a notch spacing inconsistent with any bed thickness. Running one test identifies nothing; running four leaves one cause standing.

Why it matters

Before the anomaly goes on a slide

The low-frequency shadow has been used as a direct hydrocarbon indicator, published as one, and also shown to arise from several causes that have nothing to do with fluid. Both accounts are in the literature and neither settles it.

  • Before calling an anomaly a reservoir. Four causes and four tests, all of which run on data already in hand.
  • When a thickness map has a bullseye in it. A local thickening on a spectral thickness map is often a local attenuation, and the shadow test distinguishes them.
  • When two surveys disagree. Frequency differences across a survey boundary are processing until shown otherwise, and the boundary shape usually settles it.
  • When a bed is thicker than assumed. The lobe ambiguity means a thickness estimate has more than one answer, and the band decides whether they can be told apart.
  • Reading the literature. Shadows are reported both as reliable indicators and as artifacts, and papers that state which tests were run are the ones to trust.

The mistakes that carry the most consequence. The most consequential is treating a low-frequency anomaly as a fluid indicator without checking whether it has a shadow beneath it, since the shadow is what distinguishes attenuation from tuning. The second is assuming the cause lies at the anomaly. Attenuation is cumulative, so the absorbing body can be far shallower than the level where the anomaly appears.

The AASPI documentation supplied with this module set contains no Q estimation program. This module therefore teaches what attenuation looks like and how to test for it, rather than how to measure Q. The tests in step 5 are ones an interpreter can run with the volumes they already have.

Exercises

  1. Try to identify the cause from the target alone. Why: this is the test that fails, and knowing it fails is what the rest of the module rests on. In step 1, cycle through the four locations and compare the spectra. Before cycling through them: the four spectra below have four different geological causes. How many should be distinguishable?
    HintThree of them look like a spectrum with its peak pulled down — 25.4, 25.7 and 22.6 Hz against a background of 32.4 — similar enough that a peak frequency map would contour them into one family of anomalies. The fourth reads 32.6 Hz, indistinguishable from the background, and it holds a 72 ms bed. A map of this line would show three anomalies and miss the largest body on it. Start from that position before reaching for the diagnostics: what separates these four causes is not in the picture that drew attention to them in the first place.
  2. Turn a gas sand into a thickness error. Why: attenuation produces the same spectral signature as a thin bed, so a thickness map inherits it. In step 2, take Q from 200 down to 10 and read the false thickness.
    HintThe peak falls from 29.9 Hz at Q = 200 to 26.8 at Q = 80, 20.2 at Q = 30 and 11.1 at Q = 10, and the thickness inferred from it is too large by 1.2, 3.1, 9.2 and 29.4 ms respectively, with no bed involved anywhere. The reason this matters more than it first appears is that attenuation increases with travel time, so on a real survey the effect is a systematic deepening trend on any thickness map made without correcting for it — not scatter, and not something more traces will average away.
  3. Find the shadow. Why: the shadow beneath the anomaly is what separates attenuation from tuning, and it is the one test most often skipped. In step 3, compare the frequency 200 ms below the target at the two locations.
    HintAt Q = 30 the line reads 28.3 Hz above the body, 25.8 at the target and 26.0 two hundred milliseconds below, against 28.3 above; at Q = 15 that figure below falls to 5.0 Hz while the one above holds at 27.7. Two things put it there. The wave crossed the gas sand twice, down and back up, which removes high frequencies once and for all. And the section below the sand is low-Q as well in this model, so the loss keeps building the deeper the reflection came from. Beneath the tuned bed the frequency recovers by 4.1 Hz toward the background, because a tuned bed changes its own reflection and nothing else. This one comparison rules attenuation in or out, it uses data already in hand, and it takes about a minute.
  4. Meet the lobe ambiguity. Why: the same bed under two wavelets reports two frequencies, and the map does not record which lobe it read. In step 4, read the thickness that would mimic the bed on its third lobe.
    HintThe measured peak does not keep falling as the bed thickens. It follows 1/(2T) down for a while and then jumps back up onto a higher lobe, because the wavelet has stopped illuminating the low one — a 24 ms bed reads 20.8 Hz and a 72 ms bed reads 32.6 Hz. So the thickness inverted from a peak frequency has more than one answer, and on a thick bed it returns the wrong one without any sign of trouble. What would separate them is notch spacing, since the notches sit at 1/T regardless of which lobe carries the peak — but that check needs bandwidth the data here does not have, which is why it is often unavailable exactly where it would help.
  5. Run the whole panel. Why: no single test identifies a cause; four together leave one standing. In step 5, look at which tests fire at which locations.
    HintAttenuation shows 3.2 Hz of shadow below and 0.1 Hz above, with 59% of the magnitude gone. Processing shows a very similar 3.6 Hz below, but 28.3 Hz above — a different wavelet is different at every depth. The two shadows are nearly equal, so the shadow alone decides nothing; the reading above the body is what tells them apart. The thick bed fires neither depth test but shifts 10.0 Hz when the wavelet moves, because the wavelet decides which lobe gets measured. Tuning gives 0.0, 0.0 and 1.4 Hz, so it is diagnosed by exclusion rather than by evidence. Say so when the conclusion is written down: "the other three were ruled out" is a weaker claim than "this is a tuned bed," and it is the one the data supports.

Key points

  • Three different things produce the same low-frequency anomaly: a bed at tuning, attenuation above the target, and a processing difference. None can be identified from the target spectrum alone.
  • A bed can also be too thick to see at all. When the first tuning lobe falls below the recorded band the peak lands on a higher lobe, so a 72 ms bed reads at the background frequency and a peak frequency map shows nothing where the largest reservoir is.
  • Attenuation reshapes the spectrum as well as weakening it, losing amplitude as exp(−πft/Q) so that the peak moves down and a thickness inferred from it comes out too large.
  • The shadow is the diagnostic. Attenuation affects everything recorded beneath the absorbing body; tuning affects only its own reflection. Comparing frequency well below the target rules attenuation in or out.
  • Attenuation is cumulative, so the cause may be far shallower than the anomaly, and its effect on a thickness map is a depth trend rather than scatter.
  • The wavelet decides which tuning lobe is measured, so a thickness inverted from a peak frequency has more than one answer and returns the wrong one on thick beds without warning.
  • An anomaly whose boundary follows a survey edge is processing, and that is the most direct of the four to check.

Words introduced here

low-frequency shadow
A zone of reduced peak frequency beneath a body, produced by absorption on the way down and back. The diagnostic that separates attenuation from tuning.
Q, the quality factor
How lossy a rock is. High Q means low loss. Amplitude at frequency f after travelling for time t is multiplied by exp(−πft/Q).
attenuation, or absorption
The loss itself. It removes high frequencies faster than low ones, so it changes the shape of the spectrum as well as its size.
cumulative
Absorption accumulates with travel time, so the effect grows with depth below the body and the cause can be far shallower than the anomaly.
tuning lobe
A bed produces spectral maxima at 1/(2T), 3/(2T), 5/(2T) and so on. Which one carries the most energy depends on the wavelet.
lobe ambiguity
The same bed measured under two different wavelets reporting two different peak frequencies, because a different lobe dominated.
direct hydrocarbon indicator
An observation taken as evidence of hydrocarbons. The low-frequency shadow has been published both as one and as an artifact of several other causes.

Method

What is computed, and what is left out

Attenuation is applied as a constant-Q filter in the frequency domain: amplitude multiplied by exp(−πft/Q), together with the velocity dispersion that must accompany it, referenced to 50 Hz. Dispersion means the higher frequencies travel slightly faster and so arrive slightly early; the effect is small at these values of Q and is included for correctness rather than because the module depends on it. The amplitude loss was checked against the closed form at 20, 40 and 60 Hz, and the direction of the dispersion against the exact power law, before this module was built.

How the absorbing location is built. Everything below 150 ms at that location is given the chosen Q, and the loss applied to each reflection uses the two-way time accumulated between 150 ms and that reflection. So the shadow has two parts: a step at the absorbing body, from crossing it twice, and a downward trend below it, because the rock beneath is lossy too. A model in which only a thin gas sand absorbed would give the step and no trend. Real sections show both, and the trend is normally larger, which is why the module is built this way.

The four locations are separate synthetic traces rather than positions in a lateral model, built with the same background wavelet: a bed at the chosen thickness, the same background beneath an absorbing layer, a bed three times thicker, and the background under a different wavelet. Building them separately keeps each cause isolated, which a real line would not do.

A convention used throughout this set. A tuned bed produces maxima in its spectrum at 1/(2T), 3/(2T), 5/(2T) and so on, all of similar height once the wavelet has been divided out. Taking the tallest of them makes a thickness estimate jump between lobes as the bed changes. Every module here that picks a peak frequency therefore takes the lowest-frequency maximum that reaches 70% of the tallest, which is the one that inverts to the correct thickness. Production software achieves the same thing by asking for a frequency search range.

The diagnostics in step 5 are computed, not asserted. Shadow strength is the difference in peak frequency 200 ms below the target between that location and the background. Magnitude change is the ratio of peak magnitude at the target to the background's. The wavelet test recomputes each location with the wavelet peak moved by 10 Hz and reports how far the anomaly's peak frequency moved. The notch test finds the lowest genuine notch and compares 1/(notch frequency) with the thickness implied by the peak.

Simplifications, stated plainly. There is no Q estimation here and none in the AASPI documentation this set was built against, so the module teaches recognition rather than measurement. Q is constant with frequency, which is the usual assumption and is known to be imperfect. The model has no multiples, no mode conversion, no anisotropy and no transmission losses other than the absorption itself, all of which change spectra in real data. And the four causes are shown one at a time, where a real anomaly will often have two or three of them at once — which makes the diagnostics weaker in practice than they look here.

Where this departs from production software. Nothing in this module corresponds to an AASPI program. Spectral kurtosis and skewness are sometimes used to estimate Q between two picked horizons, and narrow-band wavelets have been used for the same purpose, but neither is a program in the documentation supplied and neither is implemented here.
