Module 06  ·  Spectral attributes

Peak frequency, peak magnitude, and the maps they make

A decomposition produces a whole spectrum at every sample of every trace, which is more than anyone can look at. The standard answer is to reduce each spectrum to a single number and map that. These five steps cover which numbers are used, what each one tracks, where the maps carry values that mean nothing, and what a delivered thickness map gets wrong.

Peak magnitude and peak frequency measure different properties of one spectrum. Magnitude measures how much energy is present; frequency measures where in the band that energy sits. Walking the analysis point from the channel axis to a margin lowers the magnitude and raises the frequency, because the fill is thinning. Away from the channel both readouts still return numbers, and only the magnitude indicates that there is nothing there.

2The same reduction, everywhere

Two volumes out of one decomposition

Why this step is here. A peak frequency map of a channel looks like a thickness map. Is it one?

The section in the header is cut along the inline through the analysis point, and the point is the crimson line on it. Every number on this page was measured on a trace like the ones drawn there, in a window around the top of the fill. The maps are a reduction of those traces to one number each, and the section is what was reduced.

The workflow that makes one of these a thickness map runs as follows. The channel fill has a top and a base, so it returns two reflections separated by the fill thickness. Those two interfere, putting a peak in the spectrum at 1/(2T). Measuring where that peak sits therefore measures T. Every step has to hold for the map to mean a thickness.

Peak magnitude rests on no such sequence. It measures how much energy is present, which depends on the size of the impedance contrasts, on how close the fill is to tuning, and on the local noise. It is useful for locating a feature, and its value cannot be inverted for a thickness the way peak frequency can.

Where the workflow breaks in real rock. Each step is an assumption about the geology, and each one fails in a recognizable setting. A single top and base require the fill to be one acoustic unit; an incised valley with three stacked fills separated by flooding surfaces returns six reflections rather than two, and the spectrum holds the spacing of all of them. The interference peak has to be the lowest strong maximum; where a thin bed sits directly above the target, its own peak can be the taller one. And the peak has to lie inside the recorded band, which fails once the fill is thick enough to put 1/(2T) below the low cut. A peak frequency map over an amalgamated valley fill 60 ms thick is measuring something, and it is not the thickness of the fill.

Peak frequency tracks thickness; peak magnitude does not. Measured against the true fill over the whole channel, peak frequency correlates at −0.99 and peak magnitude at 0.21. The sign on the first is negative because thicker fill tunes lower. The second is near zero because magnitude is brightest at the tuning thickness and falls away on both sides of it, so one magnitude value corresponds to two thicknesses.

3Where a peak frequency map has no signal

Off the channel there is nothing to measure

Why this step is here. A peak frequency map has a value at every point on the survey, including where there is no reflection. What are those values?

The left map is peak frequency as computed. The right map is the same thing with every point below a magnitude threshold blanked. The difference between them is how much of the first map was describing nothing.

Both halves of this have a geological consequence. Left ungated, the floodplain either side of the channel carries frequencies drawn from whatever noise is present, and those values contour smoothly and form patterns. Interpreters have mapped crevasse splays and levee wedges out of them. Gated hard, the parts of the body with the weakest reflection disappear, and those are the margins and the downstream thin reach: the very parts a stratigrapher wants in order to place the channel edge, judge connectivity to the next body, and decide whether a splay is attached. Raising the threshold from 10% to 40% here takes the on-channel share of the surviving map from 88% to 100% and removes a fifth of what was kept.

The threshold is therefore a mapping decision rather than a display setting, and the map's outline moves with it. Two interpreters given the same volume and different thresholds will draw different channel margins. Module 07 shows the display that avoids choosing a threshold at all.

Most of an ungated peak frequency map describes noise. With no threshold the map covers 100% of its area and only 45% of that area has any channel under it. The remaining 55% carries frequency values computed from whatever was in the window, and they are as colorful and as smooth as the real ones. Modulating frequency by magnitude, which is the standard display, is how that area is made visibly absent rather than invisibly wrong.

Mean frequency reads above peak frequency almost everywhere, by 4.2 Hz on average here. A seismic spectrum has a longer tail on the high side than on the low, so the amplitude-weighted average is pulled above the mode. That is the same 1.128 ratio module 01 measured on a Ricker, appearing on real spectra. Mean frequency is steadier under noise because it uses the whole spectrum; peak frequency is sharper on a clean tuned bed because it uses only the mode.

5The delivered thickness map, and its error

Peak frequency, inverted, gated, compared with the truth

Why this step is here. A thickness map is delivered from peak frequency. How wrong is it, and is it wrong in a pattern?

The left panel is that map. The right panel is its error against the truth, which would not be available on real data and which is the reason for testing on a model where it is.

The error has a shape, and the shape follows the body. It is not scattered. With a 30 Hz wavelet the map is 4.2 ms out on average and is at its best around 16 ms of fill, within 0.6 ms of the truth. It is at its worst on the thinnest fill, 9.3 ms out at 4 ms, where the interference peak has moved above the recorded band. Changing the wavelet moves the good part of the map rather than improving it: at 40 Hz the best thickness becomes 12 ms and the worst becomes the thick fill, 20.8 ms out at 32 ms of fill.

For an interpretation this matters more than the average error does. A thickness map whose error is organized like the channel will place its own thickest values slightly off the thalweg, and will taper differently from the way the body tapers. Contoured and handed on, it produces a geologically plausible picture whose relief is partly the wavelet. The one check that is always available is to remake the map from a different wavelet or a different frequency and see which parts of it move.

Why it matters

What these maps are used for

Peak frequency and peak magnitude are the two most commonly delivered spectral attributes. Most of the errors made with them come from treating one as though it answered the other's question.

  • Reservoir thickness mapping. Peak frequency inverted through 1/(2f), tied to a well, is the standard route to a thickness map for a bed too thin to pick.
  • Finding a feature versus measuring it. Magnitude finds the channel because it responds to reflection strength; frequency measures the fill because it responds to thickness. Using magnitude as a thickness proxy is a common error, and it carries into any volume estimate built on the map.
  • Deciding where a map can be interpreted. Step 3 is the whole argument for displaying frequency modulated by magnitude rather than on its own.
  • Reading someone else's volume. Peak and mean frequency maps look alike and read differently by several hertz, which is several milliseconds of inferred thickness.
  • Quality control on a decomposition. A peak frequency map that is flat at the wavelet's own peak over most of the survey means the balancing in module 05 was not done, or not done well. Balancing is not automatically an improvement either — on data with one stationary wavelet it reduces accuracy rather than adding it, as the toggle in step 2 shows.

How a peak frequency map gets over-read. The commonest error is interpreting it where the peak magnitude is near zero, which produces a confident-looking value computed from noise. The second is inverting it to a thickness outside the working range, where the answer is biased rather than absent. Both produce maps that look like geology.

The first question to ask of a spectral attribute map is what was done about the low-magnitude areas. If nothing was, most of the map's area is describing whatever was in the window.

Exercises

  1. Walk the analysis point off the channel. Why: both readouts return a number off the channel, and only one of them shows that nothing is there. In step 1, move the crossline slider from the channel axis out past the margin, watching peak magnitude and peak frequency. Before moving it: off the channel entirely there is no reflection to measure. What should the peak frequency readout do?
    HintMagnitude falls steadily as the fill thins and the top and base reflections start cancelling each other. Peak frequency climbs as it should, tracking the thinning, and then off the channel it does not stop having a value — it jumps around, because there is no reflection left and the highest point of a noise spectrum is wherever the noise happens to be highest. That behavior is the whole of step 3. Notice how little warning there is on the readout itself: the frequency is reported to a tenth of a hertz whether or not there is anything to measure.
  2. Check which map tracks the geology. Why: the two attributes are routinely delivered together, and only one of them measures thickness. In step 2, read the two correlation numbers, then change the wavelet frequency and read them again.
    HintPeak frequency correlates −0.99 with fill thickness — thin fill, high frequency — and holds at −0.98 with a 22 Hz wavelet. Peak magnitude runs +0.73, +0.21 and −0.17 at 22, 30 and 40 Hz: weak, and not even stable in sign. With a low-frequency wavelet the thick axis is bright and the correlation is positive; with a high-frequency wavelet the margins are bright and it flips. A magnitude map is a picture of tuning, not of thickness, and its relationship to the geology is not even consistent in sign.
  3. Find how much of the map is noise. Why: this is the fraction of a delivered map that should not be interpreted at all. In step 3, take the magnitude threshold from zero upward and watch the two area readouts.
    HintAt a threshold of zero the map keeps 100% of its area and 45% of that is channel. At 10% it keeps 51% of the area and 88% is channel; at 20%, 48% and 94%; at 40%, 43% and 100%. Almost all of the area thrown away in the first step was noise, which is why the on-channel share jumps so far for so little lost. The spread readouts make the same point: 10.3 ms of scatter off the channel ungated, 3.1 ms among the points that survive. Gating removes bad values rather than improving them. There is no correct threshold — too high removes the thin margins, which are usually the part of most interest — but there is no case for zero.
  4. Compare the three reductions under noise. Why: the choice between peak and mean frequency is a choice about noise, not about correctness. In step 4, read the three correlations with no noise, then at the maximum noise setting.
    HintPeak frequency correlates −0.99 with clean data and −0.98 at 20% noise. Mean frequency manages −0.81 and −0.77 over the same range. The usual argument for mean frequency is that it averages over the band and should therefore be steadier in noise, and on this model that advantage does not appear: peak frequency stays ahead throughout, and both degrade by about the same small amount. The reason is that mean frequency's averaging includes the parts of the band that carry no thickness information at all, and that degrades the estimate more than the noise does. The gap between mean and peak also widens slightly with noise, from 4.1 to 4.9 Hz, so a mean frequency map read as a peak frequency map gets worse on worse data.
  5. Give one dark patch three explanations. Why: a dark patch on a magnitude map has several causes, and naming one without testing the others is the error this module exists to prevent. In step 2, find a place where the peak magnitude map is dark, then work out what could put it there.
    HintDark on a magnitude map means little energy at the frequency that was strongest, and several things produce that. Off the channel there is no reflection at all. Over the thalweg the fill is too thick to tune with the wavelet, so the two reflections are separating and neither is reinforced. At the very margins the fill is so thin that the top and base reflections nearly cancel. On real data add two more: a sand whose impedance happens to match the shale gives a small reflection coefficient whatever its thickness, and an absorbing body above the target removes the high frequencies before they arrive. Five causes, one appearance. The peak frequency map distinguishes some of them — thick fill reads low, thin fill reads high, and no reflection reads noise — which is the practical reason the two maps are interpreted together rather than separately.
  6. See the working range in map form. Why: module 04's working range appears here as a spatial pattern in the error, which reads as geology. In step 5, look at where the error map is worst and compare with the best and worst fill readouts.
    HintWith a 30 Hz wavelet the best fill thickness is 16 ms, where the map is 0.6 ms out, and the worst is 4 ms, where it is 9.3 ms out. At 40 Hz the sweet spot moves to 12 ms and the worst case becomes 32 ms fill at 20.8 ms out. The error is small over a band of fill thicknesses and grows at both ends — thick fill where 1/(2f) has dropped below the recorded band, and thin fill where it has climbed out of the top. That is module 04's working range, seen from above instead of in profile. Two consequences for a real deliverable. The error is a bias rather than scatter, so it does not average away with more traces. And a single-point well tie corrects it only near the thickness of that well, which is why thickness maps calibrated at a thick well tend to be wrong at the thin edge of the same body.

Key points

  • Each attribute reduces a whole spectrum to one number, and which number decides what the map can show. Peak frequency, peak magnitude, mean frequency and peak-above-average are four different questions asked of the same data.
  • Peak frequency tracks thickness; peak magnitude tracks tuning. Magnitude finds the feature, frequency measures it, and using magnitude as a thickness proxy fails in a way that is not even consistent in sign.
  • A peak frequency map has a value everywhere, including where there is no signal. Off the feature it reports the highest point of a noise spectrum, to a tenth of a hertz, with no indication that anything is wrong.
  • Frequency should be displayed modulated by magnitude, or at least gated by it. There is no correct threshold, and there is no case for none at all.
  • Mean frequency reads about 4 Hz higher than peak frequency here, because seismic spectra have a longer tail on the high side. The two maps look alike and imply thicknesses around 2 ms apart, everywhere and in the same direction.
  • Thickness error from a peak frequency map is a bias, not scatter. It grows at both ends of the working range, does not average away, and is only partly fixed by a single-well calibration.

Words introduced here

peak frequency
The frequency at which the spectrum is highest, computed at every sample. Tracks bed thickness through 1/(2T), within the working range of module 04.
peak magnitude
The height of that peak. Measures how much energy is present, and is brightest at the tuning thickness rather than at the greatest thickness.
mean frequency
The amplitude-weighted average across the spectrum. Reads about 4 Hz above peak frequency on these data, and is steadier under noise.
peak above average
How far the peak stands above the mean level of its own spectrum. A measure of how sharply tuned a spectrum is rather than of where it is tuned.
magnitude gating, or blanking
Removing points where the magnitude is too low for the frequency to mean anything. Without it, most of a map's area is computed from noise.
modulated display
Showing frequency as color and magnitude as brightness in one image, so low-magnitude areas go dark instead of showing a confident color.
bias
An error with a pattern rather than scatter. The thickness error here is a bias, and it has the shape of the channel.

Method

What is computed, and what is left out

The model is the channel from module 00 on a 96 by 80 grid: a sinuous axis, a cosine cross section thinning to zero at the margins, and a downstream taper. At each grid point the fill is a pair of reflection coefficients convolved with a zero-phase Ricker, plus band-limited noise scaled to the local reflection strength.

The spectra are direct discrete Fourier transforms of a Hann-tapered window around the channel interval, computed at every grid point. The cosine and sine tables are built once per frequency and reused across all points, which is what makes seven thousand spectra per redraw practical in a browser.

A convention used throughout this set. A tuned bed produces maxima in its spectrum at 1/(2T), 3/(2T), 5/(2T) and so on, all of similar height once the wavelet has been divided out. Taking the tallest of them makes a thickness estimate jump between lobes as the bed changes. Every module here that picks a peak frequency therefore takes the lowest-frequency maximum that reaches 70% of the tallest, which is the one that inverts to the correct thickness. Production software achieves the same thing by asking for a frequency search range.

The statistics follow the definitions in the AASPI documentation. Peak frequency is the mode, refined by fitting a parabola through the three samples around it. Mean frequency is the magnitude-weighted average over the band. The range-trimmed mean is the average magnitude between the 15th and 85th percentiles of the cumulative spectrum, and peak magnitude above average is the peak minus that. Correlations are ordinary Pearson coefficients between an attribute and the true fill thickness, computed over points where fill exists.

Balancing, when switched on, averages the power spectrum over all grid points and applies the same operator everywhere, with the prewhitening and band taper from module 05. It is stationary — one operator for the whole map — where production balancing is time-variant.

Simplifications, stated plainly. The channel is flat, so the extraction window is the same everywhere and the interval is known exactly; in real work the extraction geometry is usually the largest source of error and it is absent here entirely. The fill has one uniform impedance, which makes every point a clean two-reflector bed. There is no dip, no compaction and no lateral velocity change. The error map in step 5 uses a truth that no real survey has.

Where this departs from production software. AASPI computes these attributes from a wavelet transform or matching pursuit at every sample of a 3D volume, then extracts along an interpreted horizon or within a stratal slab; here the analysis window is fixed by the model. AASPI also computes peak phase, spectral bandwidth, slope, roughness, skewness and kurtosis from the same spectra, none of which are shown here — the landing page lists them.
