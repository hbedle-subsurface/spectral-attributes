Module 05  ·  Spectral attributes

Is that a map of the geology, or of the source wavelet?

Module 04 ended with every thickness estimate having a working range, and that range being a property of the wavelet rather than of the earth. Every reflection in a section is a copy of the same waveform, so the shape of that one waveform is present in every spectrum measured anywhere in the survey, and a map of peak frequency is partly a map of the source. Spectral balancing divides the wavelet back out. These five steps show what it corrects, what it changes about the wavelet the data carry, and one way of applying it that removes the target along with the wavelet.

1What an unbalanced peak frequency map contains

Change the wavelet and watch the "geology" move

Why this step is here. A peak frequency map shows a low-frequency zone over the reservoir. Is that the reservoir, or the wavelet?

Why the source turns up in the answer at all. Every reflection in a seismic section is the same waveform, arriving at a different time with a different size. The rocks decide the times and the sizes; the source, the recording system and the processing decide the waveform, and that waveform is the same at every reflection in the survey. So whatever shape that one waveform has in the frequency domain is present in every spectrum measured anywhere in the volume, on top of whatever the rocks contributed. A peak frequency map made from those spectra carries both, added together, with no label saying which part is which.

The test that settles it: change the wavelet peak frequency in the header without touching the earth model. The rocks are identical in every case. The estimated thickness changes.

The wavelet's own peak frequency pulls the measurement toward itself. Wherever the bed's contribution to the spectrum is weak — too thick, too thin, or too noisy — the measured peak falls back to the wavelet's peak, and a thickness derived from it is a restatement of the source. The zones where that happens are exactly the zones outside the working range module 04 measured, so the two findings are the same finding seen from opposite ends.

2The balancing operator

Divide the wavelet back out

Why this step is here. If the recorded spectrum is the earth times the wavelet, and only the product was recorded, how can the wavelet be separated out?

What an operator is. The word sounds like a piece of mathematics and it is a list of numbers. There is one number for each frequency, and applying the operator means multiplying the amplitude at that frequency by the number beside it. The operator that balances data is a list that is large at the frequencies the wavelet made weak and small at the frequencies the wavelet made strong, so that after the multiplication every frequency in the band comes out at about the same amplitude. A gain curve does the same thing to a trace against time; this one works against frequency instead.

The panels below show the average spectrum, the operator built from it, and the result of applying it. The operator is not simply one over the average, for the reason step 3 takes up.

One consequence is worth stating first. A tuned bed has maxima at 1/(2T), 3/(2T), 5/(2T) and so on, all the same height. On unbalanced data the wavelet suppresses the higher ones, so only the first is visible. Balancing restores them, and a spectrum that previously showed one peak now shows several. That is the correct answer and it looks like new structure appearing.

Balancing makes module 04's arithmetic usable. Flattening the spectrum adds no information: the notches and the tuning peak were present in the recorded data, weighted by the wavelet. What balancing removes is that weighting. The measurement that follows is of the bed rather than of the source.

3Prewhitening

Take α to its smallest and watch the edges

Why this step is here. Dividing by the average spectrum works where the spectrum has energy. What happens at the edges of the band, where it has almost none?

The operator is therefore not 1/A but closer to 1/(A + α), where α is a small fraction of the spectrum's peak. That α is prewhitening, and it sets how hard the balancing pushes at the edges of the band.

Balancing at a small α adds events to the section. One trace a quarter of the way along the wedge crosses 6 separate events on the recorded section. After balancing at α = 0.1% it crosses 10. The extra four are the sidelobes of the sharpened wavelet, and on the section they appear above and below each real reflector as events of the same character. At α = 4% the same trace crosses 4 after balancing, fewer than the recorded section, because the gentler operator has not sharpened the wavelet enough to separate the top and base of the thinning bed. Neither count matches the two reflection coefficients the model contains.
Raising α flattens the spectrum less and applies less gain. At α = 0.1% the operator's largest gain is 32.8× and the balanced spectrum's flatness is 0.005 — very flat, and every bit of that 32.8× is applied where the recorded spectrum was near zero, which is to say to noise. At α = 12% the largest gain is 6.6× and the flatness is 0.231: quieter, and less corrected. Neither end is right, which is why it is a parameter and not a constant. A note for anyone comparing these numbers with the AASPI documentation: it gives this value as 1% in one program and 2% in another, and the interface text suggests 4% would be more conservative. Those are three different numbers for the same symbol in the same equation, so do not read too much into any particular default.

4Bluing, and why flat is not the target

The earth is not white

Why this step is here. Whitening assumes the earth's reflectivity spectrum is flat. Is it?

Bluing corrects for that: after whitening, the spectrum is tilted back up by fβ. Raising β from zero tilts the balanced spectrum, and changes the wavelet alongside it.

Balancing moves the thickness at which a bed stops being measurable. On the default 28 Hz wedge the recorded section reports the correct thickness down to 12.1 ms and no further. After whitening the same wedge reports it down to 7.6 ms, so about 5 ms of section that carried no usable thickness before now does. Nothing was added to the model. Bluing at β = 0.8 takes the effective peak frequency from 31.4 Hz to 64.3 Hz and the wavelet sidelobe from 0.074 to 0.146, while the thinnest measurable bed stays at 7.6 ms: past a point the extra tilt is adding oscillation rather than resolution. The event count on the third panel rises from 6 to 14 at the default 4% noise; taking the noise slider to zero returns it to 6, which identifies those extra events as amplified noise rather than as sidelobes.

What this changes for stratigraphy. A pinch-out, an onlap, and a package of stacked thin sands are all read from where reflections separate and where they merge. That thickness belongs to the wavelet the data are carrying, and balancing replaces the wavelet. Two volumes over the same ground, one balanced and one not, will therefore place the same pinch-out at different points, and the geology has not moved between them. A bed count made on a balanced volume and a bed count made on the delivered stack are two different numbers, and the difference is not an error in either.

This is also why spectral work is not only a channel-mapping technique. Thickness is what these panels measure, and thickness is the quantity a stratigrapher is usually after, whether it is held in a channel fill, a shoreface parasequence, a carbonate buildup or a progradational wedge. The channel in module 00 is the easiest case to draw, not the only one the argument applies to.

Every step of this changes the wavelet being interpreted. Balancing is not an adjustment to a display. It convolves the data with a new wavelet, and every amplitude, every tuning thickness and every apparent bed thickness measured afterwards belongs to that new wavelet rather than to the recorded one.

5Balancing each trace on its own

Watch the target disappear

Why this step is here. Every trace has its own spectrum. Why not give every trace its own balancing operator?

It also removes the measurement. A tuned bed's whole signature is a notch — a hole in that trace's spectrum. Whitening that trace on its own is, by definition, filling the hole in.

Trace-by-trace whitening removes the geology along with the wavelet. The survey-average operator leaves 82% of the notch depth intact and a notch-based thickness estimate works on 97% of the traces. Give every trace its own operator and the notch retains 13% of its depth, the estimate on the 20 ms bed cannot be made at all, and the number of usable traces falls to 1%. The operator cannot tell a dip caused by the source from a dip caused by a 20 ms bed, because on one trace they look identical. The result is a smooth, flat, professional-looking spectrum with the measurement gone out of it. This is why spec_cwt and spec_cmp compute one operator from a survey average, and why the AASPI documentation warns against the trace-by-trace program for exactly this reason. The averaging is not a computational shortcut. It is what protects the signal.

Why it matters

Where this shows up in real work

Balancing is the step between running a spectral decomposition and being able to interpret the result. Skipping it does not produce an obviously broken answer; it produces a plausible one that is partly about the source.

  • Before any thickness estimate. Module 04's arithmetic assumes the measured peak is the bed's. On unbalanced data it is largely the wavelet's, and the resulting map is smooth, plausible and wrong.
  • Before comparing two surveys, or two parts of one. Different vintages have different source signatures, and an unbalanced peak frequency map will show the survey boundary as a geological feature.
  • Before comparing shallow with deep. The wavelet is not the same at 1 s and 3 s, so an unbalanced attribute has a depth trend built into it that has nothing to do with rocks.
  • When a volume arrives described as "whitened". Ask what α was, whether any bluing was applied, and whether it was done trace by trace. All three change what the volume means, and the third can have removed the target.
  • When a balanced section suddenly has more reflectors. Step 4 is the reason to suspect the new ones are sidelobes.

What balancing is blamed and credited for. The first error is believing it adds resolution. It removes a bias and adds nothing. The second is applying it without deciding what the reflectivity spectrum is assumed to be, since a fully whitened volume has been corrected for a wavelet that was partly the earth.

An attribute that changes when the acquisition or the processing changes, and the earth does not, is measuring the acquisition. Step 1 is that test, run on a model where the answer is known.

Exercises

  1. Change the source and watch the geology move. Why: if an attribute moves when the source changes and the rocks do not, the attribute is measuring the source. In step 1, take the wavelet peak frequency from 16 Hz to 55 Hz, reading the estimate on a 12 ms bed each time. Before changing anything: the earth model does not change at all. Should the estimated thickness change? Decide first, then find out.
    HintThe bed never changes and the estimate does: 25.8 ms at 16 Hz, 16.1 at 28, 12.1 at 40, 10.5 at 55, for a bed that is 12 ms throughout. It follows the wavelet, because wherever the bed's own peak is weak the measured peak reverts to the source's. That is the diagnostic: an attribute that moves when the acquisition changes is reporting the acquisition. Now do the same thing in step 2, with balancing switched on, and the estimate stops following the wavelet: it settles between 18 and 20 ms across the whole range of sources. Read that carefully, because it is not the same as being right. The bed is 12 ms thick, which is below the working range module 04 measured for these wavelets, so the estimate is still several milliseconds out. What balancing has removed is the part of the error that came from the source. The part that comes from the bed sitting outside the band remains, and no operator applied to the spectrum will reach it. Nothing about the earth was different in either case; the difference is entirely whether the wavelet was divided out first.
  2. Measure how much balancing is worth. Why: this is the size of the correction, measured against a model where the true answer is known. Compare the mean error in step 1 with the same readout in step 2, at each wavelet frequency.
    Hint50% down to 19% at 16 Hz, 42% down to 27% at 55 Hz, and 16% down to 14% at 28 Hz. Balancing helps most where the wavelet was worst, and barely at all where it was already reasonable. So the answer to "do I need to do this?" depends on how far the wavelet is from flat, and the flatness readout in step 2 reports that before the decomposition is run. It also never becomes perfect. The recorded bandwidth still sets hard limits at both ends, and no operator recovers a notch that was never recorded. What balancing removes is the bias toward the wavelet's own tuning thickness, not the bandwidth limit.
  3. Measure what a small α does to the sidelobes. Why: prewhitening trades flatness against gain applied to noise, and neither end of the slider is the right answer. In step 3, take the prewhitening from its largest value down to its smallest, watching the operator gain and the noise readout.
    HintThe operator's maximum gain climbs from 6.6× at α = 12% to 32.8× at α = 0.1%, while the flatness improves from 0.231 to 0.005. α is what stops the division blowing up where the spectrum is near zero. All that gain is applied at the edges of the band, where there was no signal recorded — so what it amplifies is noise, and the noise readout climbs with it. The balanced spectrum does get flatter and wider, which looks like an improvement, and it is useful being clear about why it is not: a flat spectrum built from amplified noise contains no more information about the earth than the recorded one, but it looks like it does.
  4. Watch the wavelet change as β rises. Why: balancing convolves the data with a new wavelet, so every amplitude measured afterwards belongs to that wavelet. In step 4, take β from 0 to its maximum and watch the effective wavelet.
    HintThe effective peak frequency climbs from 31.4 Hz to 64.3 Hz and the sidelobe doubles from 0.074 to 0.146, exactly as module 01's step 3 said would happen when a spectrum is widened at its high end. The practical consequence is that a balanced section will have events on it that the unbalanced section did not, sitting symmetrically above and below the strong reflectors. They are not thin beds. Anyone interpreting a heavily balanced volume for thin-bed work needs to know the sidelobe level of the wavelet they created, which is why it is worth looking at the wavelet rather than only at the spectrum.
  5. Remove the target by whitening it. Why: trace-by-trace whitening fills in the notch that carries the thickness measurement. In step 5, switch from survey average to trace by trace and compare the notch depth and the thickness estimate.
    HintThe notch keeps 82% of its depth on the survey-average operator and 13% trace by trace, and the number of traces where a thickness can still be read from a notch falls from 97% to 1%. The reason: a survey-average operator asks "what shape is common to every trace here?", and a bed that varies laterally is not common to every trace, so it survives. A trace-by-trace operator asks "what shape does this trace have?", and the answer includes the notch, so the notch is removed. The two are not different accuracies of the same thing — they are different questions, and only one of them protects what the survey was run to measure.
  6. Give one frequency difference between two surveys three explanations. Why: a frequency difference between surveys has several possible causes, and the acquisition is usually the first to check. Two vintages over the same field give different peak frequency maps at the same horizon. Use steps 1 and 5 to list what could be responsible.
    HintStep 1 is the first and most likely: different source wavelets, so the two peak frequency maps are partly maps of two different sources, and neither is a map of the rock. Step 5 gives the second: if one vintage was balanced trace by trace and the other was not, the balanced one may have had the notch — the thing carrying the thickness information — whitened away. A third is the processing sequence in general, since deconvolution, filtering and migration all change the spectrum. Only after those three have been ruled out does a real geological difference, such as fluid change or compaction between surveys, become the leading explanation. The order matters: the acquisition and processing causes are common and can be checked against the data already in hand, and the geological one is rare and cannot be confirmed without further information.

Key points

  • An unbalanced peak frequency map is substantially a map of the source wavelet. Where the bed's contribution is weak, the measured peak falls back on the wavelet's peak — the same value everywhere, which is why the resulting map looks smooth and plausible.
  • Balancing divides out an average spectrum, computed over many traces so that the reflectivity's own variation averages away and the wavelet is what remains.
  • It removes a bias rather than adding information. The notches and the tuning peak were always in the data; balancing stops the wavelet from drowning them.
  • Prewhitening α stops the division blowing up where there is no signal. Small α gives a flatter, wider spectrum and amplified noise at the band edges; there is no correct value, and the AASPI documentation quotes three different ones.
  • Bluing β exists because reflectivity is generally not white. Well-log studies find more high frequency than a flat spectrum, so fully whitened data are over-corrected in a known direction.
  • Trace-by-trace whitening can erase the target. A tuned bed's signature is a notch in that trace's spectrum, and whitening that trace alone is an instruction to fill it in.

Words introduced here

spectral balancing
Dividing the recorded spectrum by an estimate of the wavelet's spectrum, so that what remains is mostly the earth's reflectivity.
whitening
Balancing carried to a flat spectrum. Assumes the reflectivity spectrum is itself flat.
the balancing operator
The gain curve that does it, built from the average spectrum over many traces.
prewhitening, α
A small constant added to the average spectrum before dividing, so the operator does not divide by something close to zero at the edges of the band.
bluing, β
Tilting the balanced spectrum back up by fβ, because well-log reflectivity spectra rise with frequency rather than being flat.
flatness
How close a spectrum is to level, measured here as its standard deviation divided by its mean over a fixed band.
survey-average operator
One operator for all traces. Preserves the notch that carries the thickness measurement.
trace-by-trace whitening
A separate operator for each trace. Fills the notch in, and removes the measurement with it.

Method

What is computed, and what is left out

The model is the wedge from module 04 — reflection coefficients of +0.1 and −0.1 with a linearly thinning separation — convolved with a zero-phase Ricker, plus band-limited noise. Reflection times are analytic rather than rounded to the sample grid.

The operator follows the form used by AASPI's spectral programs: the power spectrum is averaged over all the traces, and each spectrum is then multiplied by the square root of the peak average power divided by the sum of the square root of the average power and α times that same peak. Bluing multiplies the result by (f/fmax)β. Both are the same functions the library uses elsewhere in this set, so the arithmetic here is the arithmetic that was verified against known answers rather than a re-implementation.

The flatness numbers are the standard deviation of the spectrum divided by its mean, measured over a fixed band rather than the whole axis — outside the recorded band a spectrum is not supposed to be flat, and including that region makes every operator look bad. Working range is measured exactly as in module 04: the thickest and thinnest beds for which the estimate is within 20% of the truth.

Simplifications, stated plainly. The balancing here is applied to spectra, not by filtering the traces and re-transforming, which is what production code does; the difference does not matter for what is being shown but it means the "balanced trace" in step 3 is reconstructed rather than genuinely re-processed. Balancing is also stationary — one operator for the whole record — where real spectral balancing is time-variant, applied in overlapping windows down the trace, precisely because the wavelet changes with depth. That time variation is a large part of why balancing matters and it is not shown here. The wavelet is zero phase throughout, so nothing here addresses phase, and no attempt is made to correct one.

Where this departs from production software. AASPI computes its average spectrum over a running window in time as well as over traces, so the operator adapts with depth. Its spectral_balance program does offer the trace-by-trace mode shown in step 5, and the documentation warns against it in the same terms used here. The reflectivity in this model is a single wedge rather than a full series, so the "average spectrum" is much closer to the wavelet than a real survey average would be, and the balancing consequently looks cleaner here than it will on real data.
