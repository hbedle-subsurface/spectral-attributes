Module 07  ·  Spectral attributes

The colored channel image, taken apart

The image most associated with spectral decomposition is a channel in red, green and blue. It is three iso-frequency maps in one frame, and several display choices sit between the data and what appears on screen. These five steps take a blend apart, measure what its colors correspond to, and show which of its features come from the earth and which from the display.

1Three maps, stacked

Each layer alone, and the three stacked

Why this step is here. A blend is three maps in one image. How are three maps combined into one picture?

Two consequences follow from stacking that follow from no other kind of map, and both are on the controls above.

A layer hides what is beneath it. On its own the 18 Hz map is bright straight down the channel axis. In the stack, with the 46 Hz layer above it, part of that axis is covered.

Taking the layer opacity to 100% carries the effect as far as it goes: wherever the top layer has any strength it covers everything beneath. Lowering the opacity brings the lower layers back through as mixed colors.

The stacking order changes the picture. Swapping which layer sits on top produces a different image from the same three maps. Nothing about the data changed.

The first three panels below are the three maps on their own, each drawn in its own layer color. The fourth is the three of them stacked, which is the map in the header. Compare any one of the solo panels with the stack: what is bright there and missing on the right has been covered by a layer above it.

A blend carries no color bar, and cannot. Three quantities are shown at once in a single color, so no one-dimensional scale describes it. Step 2 builds the nearest thing to one by measurement. Two features of a blend therefore come from the display rather than the earth: what a layer conceals, and which layer was placed on top.

2What the colors mean

A legend, built from the model and measured off it

Why this step is here. A blend is red in one place and blue in another. What thickness does each color correspond to?

The panel below builds the legend by measurement: for each fill thickness present in the model, it reads the average color the blend assigns to it.

How a blend is read stratigraphically. Because color runs with thickness, a change of hue along a body is a change of thickness along it and nothing else. A ribbon that runs red down its axis and grades to blue at its edges is a body thickest in the middle and thinning outward, which is what a channel fill or a levee does. A ribbon that stays one color along its length is uniform in thickness, which is what a fault-bounded or an erosionally truncated edge tends to look like. Two ribbons that meet and change color at the junction are two bodies of different thickness, and the junction is a contact rather than a continuous fill.

The legend is set by the wavelet and by the three frequencies chosen, so it is not transferable. With components at 18, 30 and 46 Hz the model reads reddest at 22.5 ms of fill and bluest at 11.5 ms; moving them to 14, 26 and 50 Hz moves those to 26.5 ms and 10.5 ms. The same channel changes color without changing. A blend from one survey and a blend from the next cannot be compared by hue, and a hue quoted without the three frequencies behind it is not a measurement.

The color-to-thickness relation is neither linear nor absolute. With components at 18, 30 and 46 Hz the blend is reddest on 22.5 ms of fill, greenest on 15.5 ms and bluest on 11.5 ms, close to 1/(2f) for each. Between those thicknesses the color runs through the mixtures rather than stepping. Changing any of the three frequencies moves the whole mapping, so a color read off one blend means nothing on another.

3Scaling the three maps

Same data, same frequencies, different picture

Why this step is here. Two blends of the same data look different. One shows a strong high-frequency signal and the other barely any. Which is right?

Both scalings are defensible. They produce different images of the same earth, and the difference is large.

Independent scaling promotes the weakest frequency to equal billing. At 12, 30 and 70 Hz the raw energies of the three components stand at 0.39 : 1.00 : 0.10. Scaled independently, all three reach full brightness somewhere, and the 70 Hz layer contributes as strongly as the 30 Hz one despite carrying a tenth of the energy. On a common scale the 70 Hz layer is almost invisible, which is what the data support. The first is a contrast stretch applied per color, and it is the default in most software.

4Choosing the three frequencies

There is no default worth trusting

Why this step is here. Software offers three evenly spaced frequencies by default. Is that a good choice?

The curve below is what each single frequency is worth on its own: how much of the true channel that one component covers. The three markers are the current choices.

Read as geology, the curve is the thickness distribution of the body seen through the wavelet. Each frequency covers the part of the body near its own responding thickness, so a body with a narrow range of thicknesses has a sharply peaked curve and a body with a wide range has a broad one. Choosing three frequencies is therefore choosing which three thicknesses to image, and the choice belongs to the target: a channel that deepens and thins along its length needs them spread, while an amalgamated sheet of nearly constant thickness gains little from spreading them at all. On this model the three bunched at 26, 30 and 34 Hz cover 71% of the channel and the same three spread to 12, 34 and 78 Hz cover 88%, against 67% for the best single map.

Three frequencies cover far more than one, and their spread matters more than their center. The best single frequency here covers 68% of the channel; three together cover 88%. Moving all three together up or down the band changes the total little. Bringing them close together drops it sharply, because three nearly identical maps cover what one map covers. Even spacing across the usable band is a reasonable default for that reason rather than by convention.

5Frequency on hue, magnitude on lightness

Module 06's problem, solved properly

Why this step is here. Blanking a peak frequency map below a magnitude threshold draws a hard edge that is not in the data. Is there an alternative?

The alternative puts peak frequency on hue and peak magnitude on lightness. Where there is energy the color reports the frequency; where there is none the display goes dark. No threshold is chosen and no boundary is drawn.

Why this is the display to map an edge on. Where a body thins toward its margin the reflection weakens, so the frequency estimate becomes unreliable exactly at the point the margin has to be drawn. A blanked map puts a hard line there, at a position set by the threshold rather than by the rock, and that line is very easy to pick as the edge of the body. A modulated display fades instead, so the picture shows the estimate becoming untrustworthy rather than showing a boundary. On this model the colored part of the modulated display is 96% on channel; the unmodulated map is colored everywhere and is 45% on channel.

A fade cannot be contoured, which is the honest consequence: the edge of a thinning body is not sharply located by this method, and a display that draws it sharply is adding information the data do not hold. Placing that edge takes something else — a well, an inversion, or the geometry of the system the body belongs to.

Why it matters

Reading and making these images

Blends are the form in which spectral decomposition usually reaches an audience, and much of what makes them persuasive is display rather than data.

  • Presenting a channel or a fan. The blend shows more of a body than one map, and the color carries a thickness ordering that a grayscale map cannot.
  • Reading someone else's blend. Three questions, none of them usually answered on the slide: which frequencies, how were the channels scaled, and was the data balanced first.
  • Deciding whether a color difference is geology. A color change means one component grew relative to another. On independently scaled channels, that can happen from a change in overall amplitude rather than a change in frequency content.
  • Not over-reading a dark area. Dark can mean no reflection, or fill outside the range the three frequencies cover.
  • Displaying peak frequency at all. Hue and lightness together is the right default, and peak frequency on its own is almost never appropriate.

Why a good-looking blend can be wrong. The most damaging error is treating a blend as quantitative. It has no color bar, its color-to-thickness mapping moves with the three frequencies chosen, and its scaling is usually a per-color contrast stretch. The second error is reading a dark area as absence, when a layer above it, the scaling, or a fill thickness outside all three components can each produce one.

A blend in a report carries as much display choice as data. Its three frequencies and its scaling choice are part of what it reports, in the same way a seismic section carries its polarity convention.

Exercises

  1. Take the blend apart and put it back. Why: a combined RGB image cannot be read backwards into the three maps that made it. In step 1, set all three frequencies close together, then spread them wide, watching the coverage readouts. Before spreading them: three frequencies close together are nearly the same measurement three times. How much more of the channel should that show than one map?
    HintBunched at 26, 30 and 34 Hz the three maps are nearly the same map, the blend is gray everywhere it is bright, and it covers 71% of the channel against 67% for the best single map — almost nothing gained. Spread to 18, 30 and 46 Hz it reaches 88%. Spread apart the colors separate and the coverage climbs, because each component now lights a different part of the channel. Spread too far and the outer two land where the wavelet has little energy, so they contribute noise rather than channel. The useful spread is set by the range of fill thicknesses present, which is a geological question rather than a display one.
  2. Build the color legend. Why: a blend has no color bar, and this is the nearest thing to one that can be measured. In step 2, read the three thicknesses at which the blend is reddest, greenest and bluest.
    HintWith 18, 30 and 46 Hz the peaks are at 22.5, 15.5 and 11.5 ms against tuning thicknesses of 27.8, 16.7 and 10.9 — the middle and high ones land close, and the low one falls short because this channel has almost no fill that thick. So the color sequence red to green to blue does map to thick through thin and can be read that way qualitatively. What it cannot give is a number: the mapping is nonlinear, the spacing depends entirely on which frequencies were picked, a component tuned outside the range present peaks at the thickest fill there is instead of announcing that it is out of range, and everything beyond the covered range comes out the same dark.
  3. Change the picture without changing the earth. Why: scaling and stacking order are display choices that change what a blend appears to show. In step 3, switch between the two scaling modes and compare the two coverage readouts and the energy ratios.
    HintAt 12, 30 and 70 Hz the raw energies stand at 0.39 : 1.00 : 0.10, and independent scaling rewrites them as 1.00 : 1.00 : 1.00. Coverage goes from 61% common to 93% independent on identical data. The visible result is that a weak high-frequency component becomes a confident blue feature. Under common scaling the same feature is dim or absent. Neither image is a lie, but only one of them shows whether the blue is worth believing, and it is not the one that gets used. If a blend is showing something surprising at the high-frequency end, look at that channel on its own, unscaled, before building anything on it.
  4. Choose the three frequencies on purpose. Why: the three frequencies are the most consequential choice in the display, and software picks them by default. In step 4, find the peak of the coverage curve, then compare the three current choices with the evenly spaced default.
    HintThe best single frequency is 22 Hz at 68% coverage, and any sensible three beat it by a wide margin — 88% for 18, 30 and 46 Hz. The surprise is that the evenly spaced default of 14, 46 and 78 Hz scores 91%, and at a 40 Hz wavelet it reaches 98% against 89%. That looks like an argument for the default until step 3 applies here: coverage is counted after each channel is stretched to its own maximum, and the widest spread gains most from that stretch because its outer components carry the least energy. What the wide default really does is amplify two weak components until they look like data. So the answer is not "spread as wide as possible" — it is to pick frequencies whose tuning thicknesses straddle the expected thickness range, and to check the components at their own amplitudes before trusting a coverage number.
  5. Compare the two ways of showing peak frequency. Why: blanking draws an edge that is not in the data; modulating by magnitude does not. In step 5, read the two on-channel percentages.
    HintThe unmodulated map is colored over 100% of its area and 45% of that has channel beneath it. The hue-lightness version shows color over 47% of the area, and 96% of that is channel, with 97% of the off-channel pixels fading to dark unprompted. The rest has gone dark rather than been cut off at a contour, so there is no artificial boundary and no threshold to defend. This is why AASPI displays peak frequency this way and why a peak frequency map on its own, with a full color range applied to every pixel, should be treated as a work in progress, not a result.
  6. Give one dark area on a blend three explanations. Why: a dark area has several causes, and only one of them is absence of the feature. In step 4, find a dark region inside the channel outline, then move the three frequencies and watch whether it fills in.
    HintDark on a blend means all three components are weak there, and that happens for several distinct reasons. The fill may be thicker than the lowest component can tune, which is the case in the thalweg on a high-frequency blend. It may be thinner than the highest component can tune, which is the case at the margins and downstream. There may be no channel there at all. Or the three frequencies may simply have been chosen badly, leaving a gap in thickness coverage between them. The first two and the fourth all fill in when the frequencies move; the third does not. That test takes one drag of a slider and it is the reason a blend should never be interpreted from a single choice of three frequencies.
  7. Show that a layer hides what is under it. Why: concealment is a property of the display, and it is invisible unless it is looked for. In step 1, leave all three layers on and find a place inside the channel that looks plain blue. Now switch the blue layer off with the button in the header. Before doing so: is that pixel blue because the low and middle frequencies are absent there, or because they are covered up?
    HintBoth happen, in different places, and the blend does not distinguish them. Out on the thin margins the red and green layers really are nearly empty, so switching blue off leaves almost nothing behind. Closer in, where the fill passes through more than one component's range, removing blue reveals green underneath that was there the whole time and simply covered. This is the difference between "nothing at that frequency" and "something, hidden," and no single blended image separates them. Then swap the stacking order with the Red on top button and watch the picture change without a single number in the data changing. Anything that survives both orderings is a property of the earth; anything that does not is a property of the display.

Key points

  • A blend is three iso-frequency maps stacked as layers. Each has a color and an opacity that follows its own magnitude. Nothing is computed by combining them, so a blend shows three maps at once and is not a fourth measurement.
  • A layer covers what is under it, and the stacking order changes the picture. Where the top layer is opaque the layers beneath contribute nothing to that pixel, so a color can mean "only this frequency is present here" or "the others are hidden," and the image does not say which. Reordering the layers gives a different picture from identical data. Anything that survives a reordering belongs to the earth; anything that does not belongs to the display.
  • It covers more of a body than any single map, because three tuning thicknesses span more of the thickness range than one. It adds coverage, not resolution.
  • Color maps to thickness, but not linearly and not absolutely. The reddest, greenest and bluest thicknesses are the tuning thicknesses of the three frequencies — except where the body has no fill that thick, in which case the component peaks at the thickest fill present and gives no sign that it is out of range. Everything beyond the covered range is the same dark.
  • Independent channel scaling is a contrast stretch per color that promotes a weak component to equal brightness. It changes which features look convincing and it is almost never reported.
  • Three frequencies beat one by a wide margin — 88% coverage against 67% here — but a coverage score measured on independently scaled channels rewards wide spreads whether or not the data support them.
  • Peak frequency should be shown with magnitude on lightness, so the color fades where the confidence does, rather than being cut off at a threshold or shown everywhere.

Words introduced here

RGB blend
Three iso-frequency maps shown as one image, conventionally a low frequency in red, a middle one in green and a high one in blue.
layer, and stacking order
Each map is painted over the ones before it. Which map goes on top changes the image, and a layer conceals what lies beneath it.
opacity
How much of the layer beneath shows through. At 100% the top layer covers everything it touches.
independent scaling
Normalizing each map by its own maximum, so all three reach full brightness somewhere. A contrast stretch applied per color, and the default in most software.
common scaling
Normalizing all three by one maximum, so a weak component stays weak on screen.
coverage
The fraction of the true feature a map lights above a fixed threshold. Used here to compare one frequency against three.
hue and lightness display
Peak frequency shown as color and peak magnitude as brightness, so areas with no signal go dark without a threshold being chosen.
corender
The general name for showing two or more attributes in one image by giving each a different visual property.

Method

What is computed, and what is left out

The model is the channel from modules 00 and 06 on a 96 by 80 grid, with the same sinuous axis, cosine cross section and downstream taper. At every grid point the fill is two reflection coefficients convolved with a zero-phase Ricker, plus band-limited noise.

How the blend is composited, and the other convention. Each of the three maps is normalized to 0–1 the way step 3 describes, then painted as a layer over an ink background with an opacity equal to that normalized value: out = out (1 − a) + color × a, with a the layer's own value times the opacity set in the header. Layers are painted in the order set there too, so the last one painted sits on top. This is what a co-render does, and it is why the order control changes the picture. The opacity defaults to 70% rather than 100% because at full opacity the top layer covers the others wherever it has any strength at all, which on this channel means almost everywhere; the solo panels in step 1 are always drawn opaque, since each is a map rather than a layer in a stack.

The other convention in common use writes the three maps straight into the display's red, green and blue channels. That model is purely additive: the contributions add, stacking order is irrelevant, and a pixel strong in all three comes out white instead of taking the color of whichever layer is on top. Neither is more correct and interpretation packages differ, but the two do not produce the same image from the same three volumes, which is a practical reason to say which one a figure came from. The shared library carries both: SPEC.overlayBlend for the layered version used here, and SPEC.rgbBlend for the additive one.

The maps are computed at every point rather than interpolated from a few: each iso-frequency map is a direct discrete Fourier transform of the Hann-tapered channel window, evaluated at that one frequency. The peak frequency and peak magnitude maps in step 5 use the full spectrum and the same definitions as module 06, including the lowest-strong-lobe rule.

A convention used from module 05 onward. A tuned bed produces maxima in its spectrum at 1/(2T), 3/(2T), 5/(2T) and so on, all of similar height once the wavelet has been divided out. Taking the tallest of them makes a thickness estimate jump between lobes as the bed changes. Modules 05 through 09 therefore take the lowest-frequency maximum that reaches 70% of the tallest, which is the one that inverts to the correct thickness. Production software achieves the same thing by asking for a frequency search range. Module 04 is deliberately the exception: it takes the tallest maximum wherever it falls, so that the estimator can be watched failing at both ends of its working range, which is the point of that module's third step.

The color legend in step 2 is measured, not drawn. Fill thicknesses are binned in 1 ms steps, and for every bin the average red, green and blue of all the pixels in that bin is accumulated. The strip is that average, bin by bin, so it is the mapping this particular blend actually produces rather than an idealization of what a blend does.

Coverage counts the fraction of the true channel where a map exceeds 70% of its own maximum, with the same threshold and denominator everywhere on the page so the numbers can be compared with each other. For the blend, a pixel counts if any of the three channels exceeds it. Hue-lightness uses the conversion in assets/spectral.js, with hue running from magenta through red as frequency rises and lightness proportional to peak magnitude.

Simplifications, stated plainly. No balancing is applied, so the relative energy of the three components carries the wavelet's shape, which step 3 relies on and which would need module 05's treatment before any of this was quantitative. The channel is flat, so the extraction window is identical everywhere; in real work the extraction is usually the dominant source of error. There is no opacity or corendering with a structural attribute, which is how most production blends are actually displayed.

Where this departs from production software. AASPI's corender and hlplot work on 3D volumes along interpreted horizons and offer more control than the two scaling modes here — including separate clip percentiles per channel, which is independent scaling with more parameters rather than a different idea. Production blends are also commonly made from balanced volumes and often from more than three components using principal component reduction, which this module does not cover.
