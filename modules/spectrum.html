Module 01  ·  Spectral attributes

What a seismic wavelet is made of

Seismic data are usually described by a single number, such as "30 Hz data." That number summarizes a range of frequencies that arrive together. The width and shape of the range determine how thin a bed the data can resolve and how much the wavelet oscillates after each reflection. These five steps construct a wavelet from its frequencies and then separate it again.

1One wavelet in time and in frequency

Time on the left, frequency on the right

Why this step is here. A processing report gives the data as 30 Hz. A well tie gives 24 Hz. An attribute volume reports 34 Hz at the same location. What quantity is each of those numbers measuring?

The panel above the tabs shows one wavelet in two representations. On the left is its shape in time, the wavelet returned by each reflector. On the right is its amplitude spectrum, which gives the amount of each frequency the wavelet contains. The two representations hold the same information, and either can be computed from the other without loss.

Moving the peak frequency slider changes both representations together. A wavelet that becomes shorter in time becomes wider in frequency. This relationship holds for every wavelet and is the basis of module 02.

Peak frequency and mean frequency are two different measurements of the same spectrum. The 30 Hz Ricker has a peak at 30.0 Hz and a mean of 33.9 Hz. Its spectrum rises steeply from zero and falls away slowly, so the amplitude-weighted average lies above the most common value. For a Ricker the ratio is fixed at 2/√π ≈ 1.128 at any peak frequency. A processing report giving 30 Hz and an attribute volume reporting 34 Hz at the same sample can both be correct, because they report different statistics of one spectrum. Module 06 computes both.
Frequency becomes a length in the ground when divided into a velocity. Frequency by itself is a property of the wavelet. Velocity divided by frequency gives a wavelength: at 2500 m/s a 30 Hz wavelet has a wavelength of about 83 m, and a 15 Hz wavelet about 167 m. Bed thicknesses are compared against that length rather than against the frequency. Velocity increases with depth and the earth absorbs the higher frequencies during propagation, so wavelengths at depth are longer and the resolvable bed thickness is greater. Module 04 converts wavelength into a thickness limit, and the seismic resolution modules treat it in detail.

The sentence above about depth is the one thing on this page that cannot be seen from the panels so far, so here it is on its own. The same source wavelet is propagated to four depths through rock of the chosen quality factor Q, which measures how lossy the rock is. Absorption removes high frequencies faster than low ones, so the wavelet that arrives back from a deep reflector is not the wavelet that left.

The same survey resolves a thicker bed at depth than it does near the surface. The thickness readouts are 1/(2f) at the peak frequency that actually arrives, converted at the velocity on the slider above. Lowering Q pulls the deep spectrum down and the deep thickness limit up. Raising Q to a few hundred, which is what a stiff well-cemented section might reach, brings the four spectra almost back together. Nothing about the source changed in any of these cases, and nothing about the bed did either.

2Building the wavelet from cosines

Add components one at a time

Why this step is here. A trace is often described as containing 30 Hz. A trace is a single continuous curve, with no separable 30 Hz portion. In what sense does it contain a frequency?

The statement is arithmetic. Any wavelet can be decomposed into cosines of different frequencies, each with its own amplitude and its own shift in time. Summing those cosines reproduces the original shape exactly. The spectrum is the list of amplitudes required.

The gray curve below is the wavelet from the header. The crimson curve is the sum of the strongest few cosines only. Adding components one at a time shows which parts of the shape each frequency supplies. The first component is a single low-frequency oscillation. The narrow central peak forms only once the higher frequencies are included.

The cosines themselves, and their sum

Each row is one cosine; the bottom row is what they add up to

Each row is one cosine of a single frequency, drawn at its own amplitude and its own shift in time, with the strongest at the top. Above twelve components the rows would be too thin to read, so the rest are added together and drawn as one row. Beneath the rule are the sum, in crimson over the target wavelet in gray, and what is still missing from it. Those two are drawn against the height of the target rather than against their own, so the sum growing toward the wavelet is visible rather than scaled away.

Most of the wavelet's shape comes from the combination of components rather than from the strongest ones. On a 5-10-60-70 Ormsby, the strongest single component carries 3.6% of the energy, the strongest five carry 17.9%, and twenty are needed to exceed 70%. As components are added the central peak does not grow taller; it grows narrower. The added high-frequency cosines are in phase at the center and out of phase away from it, and that cancellation is what makes the wavelet compact. It also explains why a single frequency extracted from the data does not resemble the data. Module 03 examines one such component.

3The high cut and the corner taper

Fixed axes, so the change is real

Why this step is here. Two reprocessed volumes both have wider spectra than the original. One resolves thinner beds; the other contains reflections that are not in the ground. What distinguishes them?

The two sliders change the spectrum in different ways. The first is the high cut, the frequency above which nothing is retained. Raising it narrows the wavelet's central lobe, which is what allows two closely spaced reflectors to remain separate. Lowering it widens the central lobe until close reflections merge.

The second slider is the corner taper, which sets how gradually the spectrum falls to zero at its edges. Holding the passband fixed and making the corners sharper leaves the central lobe almost unchanged and adds a long oscillating tail to the wavelet. Both panels use fixed axes, so any change visible on screen is a real change.

Raising the high cut narrows the central lobe; sharpening the corners lengthens the tail. Taking the high cut from 25 Hz to 110 Hz narrows the central lobe from 13.3 ms to 4.1 ms and shortens the whole wavelet from 176 ms to 98 ms. Returning the high cut to 60 Hz and tightening the taper instead changes the central lobe by half a millisecond, 6.9 ms to 7.4 ms, while the ringing sidelobe rises from 0.025 to 0.054 and the wavelet lengthens from 120 ms to 303 ms. The passband is identical in both cases. Each oscillation on that tail appears in the data at the position of a reflector, so a sharpened spectrum produces events that have no counterpart in the ground.

4Bandwidth in hertz and in octaves

Same width in Hz, moved up the spectrum

Why this step is here. Two wavelets are both 30 Hz wide. One spans about 8 to 48 Hz, the other about 58 to 98 Hz. Their bandwidths in hertz are equal. Are the two wavelets equally useful?

The slider moves a band of fixed width in hertz up the spectrum. The bandwidth readout changes very little across the whole range. An octave is a doubling of frequency, and the number of octaves the band spans falls as the band moves upward: 8 to 48 Hz is more than two doublings, while 58 to 98 Hz is less than one.

The width of the spectrum sets how narrow the central peak of the wavelet is. The number of octaves it spans sets how much the wavelet oscillates on either side of that peak. The two are separate properties, and moving a band of constant width upward holds the first roughly fixed while reducing the second, which is why the wavelet in the left panel develops a longer and longer tail as the slider moves.

What the tail does on a section. The oscillations that follow a reflection arrive at a spacing set by the center frequency of the data, and that spacing is the same everywhere, so the oscillations are laterally continuous. On a section they have the appearance of beds: parallel to the reflection above them, conformable with it, and traceable across the survey. A single sand-shale interface can therefore be mapped as a package of thin units, and those units will follow structure faithfully because they are attached to the event that produced them. Distinguishing them from geology is a matter of comparing their spacing with the period of the data rather than of inspecting them at higher gain.

The same arithmetic applies with depth. Absorption removes the high end of the band preferentially, so a deep reflector is imaged mostly by what the low end carries. A survey that recorded little below 8 Hz spans few octaves at depth however it is processed, and the wavelet that comes back from a deep target rings for that reason rather than because of anything in the rocks around it.

The same bandwidth in hertz spans fewer octaves at a higher center frequency, and the wavelet oscillates more. At a band center of 28 Hz the wavelet spans 2.12 octaves and its ringing sidelobe is 0.103 of its peak. At 78 Hz it spans 0.66 of an octave and the sidelobe is 0.700, so each reflection is followed by oscillations at seven tenths of its own amplitude. Bandwidth over the same range changes only from 35.0 Hz to 34.5 Hz. The high-frequency wavelet does have the narrower central lobe, 3.2 ms against 8.9 ms, so it separates close reflectors better, and the oscillating tail comes with it. An octave is added with the smallest change in frequency at the low end: taking a low cut from 10 Hz to 5 Hz adds a full octave, while adding 5 Hz above a 60 Hz high cut adds about a tenth of one. That is the basis of broadband acquisition.

5Nyquist and aliasing

What the sample interval decides

Why this step is here. A decomposition requires a highest frequency. The data were recorded at a 4 ms sample interval. Is 130 Hz a valid request?

Seismic traces are recorded as values at regular intervals; 4 ms and 2 ms are the usual ones. A sampled trace cannot represent a frequency above half the sampling rate. That limit is the Nyquist frequency: 125 Hz at a 4 ms sample interval, 250 Hz at 2 ms.

What aliasing is. A sampled trace holds the value of the wave at the sample times and holds nothing at all about what happened between them. When the wave completes more than half a cycle from one sample to the next, more than one frequency passes through every recorded point, and the trace cannot distinguish them. The lowest of those frequencies is what is stored. Moving the tone above the Nyquist frequency shows the sampled points falling on a curve quite different from the true one, and the recorded trace is that curve.

Nothing marks an aliased value as suspect. It has an ordinary amplitude, an ordinary frequency, and it stacks and migrates like any other event. This is why an anti-alias filter is applied before data are resampled rather than afterward: once the sampling has been done the two frequencies are the same number and no processing separates them again.

The same limit applies across a survey, not only down a trace. Traces are samples in space, spaced by the bin size, so there is a spatial Nyquist frequency as well. A dipping reflector shifts in time from one trace to the next, and where that shift exceeds half a period the reflector appears to dip the other way. Steep salt flanks, fault planes and the limbs of tight folds are where this appears first, which is why a survey laid out for a flat-lying target images a steep one badly whatever is done to it later.

Where this constrains the modules ahead. A decomposition asked for a frequency above Nyquist returns a volume, because the arithmetic does not fail. What comes back is whatever the interpolation put there, it varies smoothly across the survey, and a map of it has the appearance of geology. Data recorded at 4 ms carry nothing above 125 Hz, and the highest decomposition frequency worth requesting is well below that, because absorption has usually removed the top of the band long before the sampling limit is reached.

Why it matters

Where this shows up in real work

No attribute is computed in this module. The vocabulary introduced here is used in every module that follows, and several of the parameters those modules require are determined by it.

  • Reading a processing report. "Bandwidth 8–80 Hz" and "dominant frequency 35 Hz" describe the same data and answer different questions. The first says what the data can resolve and how much the wavelet rings; the second is one statistic of the curve.
  • Merging two surveys. Two volumes with the same dominant frequency and different bandwidths will not tie, and the mistie at the seam can be mistaken for a geological change.
  • Judging a brightening. An amplitude change can come from the rocks or from a wavelet with a longer tail produced by a different processing sequence. Step 3 is where that tail comes from.
  • Choosing frequencies for a decomposition. Every program in modules 05 through 08 asks for a lowest and a highest frequency. Nyquist sets the hard ceiling; the recorded bandwidth sets the useful one.

Two points that are commonly confused. The first is treating dominant frequency as a property of the rocks. It is a property of the wavelet that traveled down and back, and it changes with depth, with the overburden, with the processing sequence and with the recording geometry. The second is assuming that a wider spectrum is always better. Widening a spectrum by sharpening its edges, which is the effect of unconstrained whitening, adds no resolution and adds oscillation. Module 05 demonstrates this.

Disagreements about the frequency content of a dataset often reduce to two statistics of the same spectrum, measured over different windows.

Exercises

  1. Compare peak frequency with mean frequency. Why: two attribute volumes can report different frequencies at the same sample, and both can be correct. In step 1, set the Ricker peak frequency to 15, then 30, then 60 Hz, and read both numbers each time. Before looking: will the mean sit above the peak, below it, or in the same place? Commit to an answer and to a reason.
    HintPeak reads 15.0, 30.0 and 60.0 Hz; mean reads 16.9, 33.9 and 67.7 Hz. The ratio is 1.128 every time, which is 2/√π. A Ricker spectrum is not symmetric — steep on the low side, a long tail on the high side — so the average always sits above the most common value. Two consequences. A mean frequency volume and a peak frequency volume will never agree on real data. And "35 Hz" does not pin down a wavelet, because a Ricker with a 31 Hz peak has a 35 Hz mean.
  2. Build a wavelet one component at a time. Why: the narrowness of a wavelet comes from interference between many frequencies rather than from the amplitude of any one of them. In step 2, step the component count from 1 up to 40 and note where the shape becomes recognizable, and what part of the curve is changing.
    HintOne component captures 3.6% of the energy and is a slow swell. Five reach 17.9%, ten reach 35.8%, twenty reach 71.2%, and it takes forty to close the last of the error. The central peak does not get taller as components are added — it gets narrower, because the new high-frequency cosines arrive in phase at the center and out of phase everywhere else. That cancellation is what makes a wavelet compact.
  3. Widen the spectrum two ways and compare the results. Why: this is the test that separates a genuine resolution gain from a lengthened wavelet. In step 3, first take the high cut from 25 to 110 Hz with the taper at 10 Hz. Then put the high cut back to 60 Hz and take the taper from 10 Hz down to 1 Hz.
    HintThe first change pulls the central lobe from 13.3 ms to 4.1 ms, better than a factor of three. The second moves it by half a millisecond, 6.9 to 7.4 ms, but takes the ringing sidelobe from 0.025 to 0.054 and the wavelet's 1% length from 120 ms to 303 ms. The passband is identical in both. The difference is between widening a spectrum and sharpening one: the useful question about a reprocessing is whether the central lobe got narrower or whether the tail got longer.
  4. Compare bandwidth in hertz with bandwidth in octaves. Why: hertz alone does not indicate whether a wavelet is broadband. Slide the band center from 28 Hz to 78 Hz and watch the bandwidth readout against the octave readout and the ringing sidelobe.
    HintBandwidth holds at 35.0, 35.0, 35.0 and 34.5 Hz across the whole slide. Octaves fall from 2.12 to 1.70 to 1.10 to 0.66, and the ringing sidelobe climbs from 0.103 to 0.181 to 0.355 to 0.700. So two wavelets of equal bandwidth in hertz are not equally good, and the octave count is what tracks the difference. An octave is added most easily at the low end, which is why broadband acquisition extends the low frequencies.
  5. Record a frequency above the Nyquist limit. Why: an alias is indistinguishable from signal in the sampled trace, which is why the filter is applied before resampling. In step 5, set the sample interval to 4 ms and walk the true frequency from 60 Hz up to 240 Hz.
    HintBelow 125 Hz the samples follow the true wave and the reported frequency is correct. At 125 Hz — Nyquist — there are two samples per cycle and it is already marginal. Above it the reported frequency comes back down: 150 Hz reports as 100 Hz, 200 Hz as 50 Hz, and 240 Hz as 10 Hz. Nothing in the sampled trace flags any of it.

If you remember nothing else

  • A wavelet carries a range of frequencies. Peak, mean and dominant frequency are each summaries of that range, and any one of them leaves most of the spectrum undescribed.
  • Peak and mean frequency are different numbers and both are correct. For a Ricker the mean sits 12.8% above the peak, always.
  • A wavelet that is short in time is wide in frequency. The two representations always change in opposite directions. Module 02 is built on that relationship.
  • Resolution comes from the high cut; oscillation comes from the corner taper. Raising the high cut narrows the central lobe. Sharpening the corners does not, and adds a tail that appears in the data as reflectors.
  • Broadband is measured in octaves. The same bandwidth in hertz spans fewer octaves at a higher center frequency and produces a wavelet that oscillates more. Octaves are gained at the low end of the spectrum.
  • Nyquist sets an absolute upper limit. At a 4 ms sample interval it is 125 Hz, and a 150 Hz event is recorded as 100 Hz, indistinguishable from signal.

Words introduced here

amplitude spectrum
How much of each frequency a wavelet or a trace contains, plotted against frequency.
peak frequency
The frequency at which the amplitude spectrum is highest.
mean frequency
The average frequency across the spectrum, weighted by amplitude. Above the peak whenever the spectrum has a long high-frequency tail.
dominant frequency
Used loosely for whichever of the two above a speaker has in mind. Worth asking which.
magnitude
How much energy sits at a given frequency. Used interchangeably with the height of the amplitude spectrum at that frequency; module 03 gives it a precise meaning once the spectrum is computed everywhere.
bandwidth
The width of the spectrum. Here, the range between the half-magnitude points either side of the peak. Other definitions are in use; module 06 meets a second one.
low cut, high cut
The frequencies below and above which nothing is kept.
taper
How gradually the spectrum falls to zero at a cut. A sharp taper produces a long wavelet.
octave
A doubling of frequency. 5 to 10 Hz is one octave; so is 40 to 80 Hz.
ringing sidelobe
The bump following the wavelet's first trough. Large sidelobes appear in data as events that are not reflectors.
Nyquist frequency
Half the sampling rate. The highest frequency a sampled trace can represent.
aliasing
A frequency above Nyquist reappearing as a lower one that was never recorded.
wavelength
Velocity divided by frequency. A length in the ground, which is what bed thicknesses get compared against.

Method

What is computed, and what is left out

How the pictures are made. The Ricker and Ormsby wavelets are evaluated in closed form from assets/seismic.js, sampled at 0.5 ms. Every amplitude spectrum on this page is a direct discrete Fourier transform of that sampled wavelet, normalized so its largest value is 1. Nothing is drawn from a stored curve, so an error in the arithmetic would show up in the picture.

The numbers in the readouts. Peak frequency is the mode of the spectrum, refined by fitting a parabola through the three samples around it. Mean frequency is the amplitude-weighted average. Bandwidth is measured between the half-magnitude points either side of the peak, which is the definition AASPI's spec_cwt uses; module 06 meets a second, incompatible definition based on percentiles, used by spec_cmp. Central lobe half-width is the time from the wavelet's center to its first zero crossing. The ringing sidelobe is the largest excursion after the first trough, as a fraction of the peak. Wavelet length is the last time at which the wavelet exceeds 1% of its peak. Wavelength is velocity divided by peak frequency at a fixed 2500 m/s, which is a round number for clastic section at a few kilometers and not a measurement of anything.

The three survey presets set the Ricker peak frequency to 15, 30 and 55 Hz. They are illustrative of deep, ordinary and shallow targets, and real surveys range well outside them; nothing in the module depends on the labels.

Step 2's reconstruction takes a real FFT of the sampled wavelet, sorts the components by amplitude, and adds back the strongest N. That ordering is a teaching choice rather than a standard: it makes the shape assemble as quickly as possible. Adding components in frequency order gives the same final answer more slowly. The energy percentage is the summed squared amplitude of the components used over that of all of them.

Step 5's alias is computed rather than drawn. The cosine is sampled at the chosen interval, and the reported frequency is found by folding the true frequency about Nyquist as many times as needed. The teal curve is the lowest-frequency cosine passing through every sample point, which is what later processing would take the data to contain.

Simplifications, stated plainly. Every wavelet here is zero phase and symmetric, which real source wavelets are not; phase is treated in module 03. There is no earth in this module — no reflectivity, no noise, no attenuation, no propagation — so the spectrum on screen belongs to the wavelet alone. Spectra are normalized to a peak of 1, so nothing here describes absolute amplitude. The anti-alias filter that precedes real resampling is deliberately absent from step 5, because the aliasing is the thing being shown.

Where this departs from production software. Nowhere yet. Nothing in this module is an algorithm; it is arithmetic that any package performs identically. The departures start in module 03, and each module from there carries its own list.
