One channel, imaged four different ways

A seismic amplitude map responds most strongly to beds of one particular thickness, set by the wavelet. Parts of a channel thicker or thinner than that produce a weaker response. Selecting a different frequency changes which thickness responds most strongly, and so changes which part of the channel appears bright. This module builds a channel of known shape, images it with the full bandwidth, then images it one frequency at a time.

1The frequencies a survey records

A narrow band, and the bed thicknesses that go with it

Why this step is here. A target sand is expected to be about 10 m thick. Can the existing survey resolve a bed of that thickness?

Seismic data occupy a narrow band of frequencies. A good marine dataset might carry useful energy from roughly 8 to 80 Hz, and older land data often less. Nothing below the low end was ever recorded, and the high end is absorbed by the earth on the way down and back.

The panel shows the amplitude spectrum of the source wavelet. The shaded region is its bandwidth: the range of frequencies over which the wavelet carries at least half its peak amplitude. The scale beneath converts each frequency to the bed thickness that frequency responds to most strongly, which is 1/(2f) in two-way time. Module 04 derives that relation. Changing the wavelet peak frequency shifts the whole scale.

The recorded band sets the range of bed thicknesses a survey can respond to. A 30 Hz Ricker carries useful amplitude between about 14 and 49 Hz. Those frequencies correspond to beds of 10.2 to 35.0 ms two-way time, or 13 to 44 m of rock at 2500 m/s. Setting the wavelet to 45 Hz shifts the range to 8.5 to 29.0 m. Beds outside the range still produce reflections, but less of the wavelet's energy lies at the frequency that suits them, so they appear weaker on a map. Separating the band into individual frequencies is how those beds are recovered.

2The channel on a full-bandwidth amplitude map

What a conventional extraction produces

Why this step is here. An amplitude map shows a bright ribbon running along a channel. Does that ribbon mark where the sand is thickest?

The model is a sinuous channel cut into shale and filled with sand. The fill is thickest along the thalweg, the channel axis, and thins toward both margins and downstream. All three of those are thickness variations, which is why a channel is used here: the geology is itself a thickness map, and the header panel shows the true one.

What the model contains. A seismic reflection comes from a contrast in acoustic impedance — a rock's density multiplied by the velocity of sound in it. Sand and shale usually differ in both, so the top and the base of the sand are two contrasts of opposite sign. Each sends back a copy of the source wavelet, and the recorded trace is the sum of those two copies. The reflection coefficient is the fraction of the arriving amplitude that returns; here it is ±0.1, a strong but ordinary sand-in-shale contrast. Everything in these modules follows from what happens when the two copies overlap.

Thickness is quoted in milliseconds because that is what the seismic measures. Seismic time is two-way time: the wave travels down through the fill and back up, so a bed delays the second reflection by twice its thickness divided by its velocity. A 24 ms fill at 2500 m/s is about 30 m of rock, not 60 m. Converting to meters needs a velocity, which is a separate measurement.

The amplitude on this map is a statement about thickness. Every trace in the model comes from the same two rocks. The reflection coefficient is +0.1 at the top of the fill and −0.1 at its base, at every point on the map, and nothing about the lithology or the pore fluid varies anywhere in it. The only quantity that changes from trace to trace is the separation in time between the two reflections. That separation alone produces every bright and dark patch on the amplitude panel. Interference between a top and a base at a particular spacing is called tuning, and the amplitude it produces is a tuning amplitude.

Amplitude measured on real data is not that pure. It carries the size of the impedance contrast at the two boundaries as well, and that contrast does depend on lithology, porosity and pore fluid. The two contributions arrive added together as one number, so a bright amplitude is ambiguous by construction: it is consistent with a thickness at tuning, with a large contrast, and with both. Reading an amplitude as a fluid effect means accounting first for the thickness effect that the geometry produces on its own. Separating the two is what the rest of this set is about.

The section in the header is cut along the profile line drawn on the map beside it, and can be moved by dragging that line. It shows the same fill as a body with a top and a base rather than as a color, which is where the thickness that produces the amplitude can be seen directly.

The brightest part of the amplitude map marks a particular fill thickness rather than the thickest fill. The thalweg holds 25.4 ms of fill. With a 30 Hz wavelet the strongest response comes from fill 13.0 ms thick, which lies out on the flanks. At 18 Hz the strongest response comes from 21.7 ms of fill, closer to the axis; at 45 Hz it comes from 8.7 ms, near the margins. The channel model is identical in all three cases. A bright ribbon can therefore mark the thalweg, a margin, or a downstream reach that has thinned into the responding thickness, and the amplitude map alone does not distinguish them.

3The same channel at three frequencies

Move the frequency slider and watch the bright part travel

Why this step is here. Two interpreters map the same channel from the same survey and produce different outlines, both working carefully. What accounts for the difference?

Each map below is the amplitude of one narrow frequency component, taken over the same interval, from the same data. No new data were acquired and no processing improved anything. The only difference between the three is which part of the spectrum was used.

Changing the frequency in the header moves the brightest part of the channel from the thalweg toward the margins.

The crimson marks on the header map show where the fill is at the thickness the chosen frequency responds to, and the ticks beneath the header section show the same positions on the profile. As the frequency rises those marks migrate outward from the axis toward the margins, and the bright ribbon on the corresponding map migrates with them. The channel underneath is unchanged throughout. This is the observation Partyka, Gridley and Lopez set out in 1999, and the reason a channel appears to change shape between one iso-frequency map and the next.

Each frequency component is strongest over a different fill thickness. At 25 Hz the strongest response comes from fill 19.2 ms thick, at 40 Hz from 12.8 ms, and at 60 Hz from 8.7 ms. Those measurements fall within a few percent of 1/(2f), which predicts 20.0, 12.5 and 8.3 ms. The low-frequency map is brightest over the thalweg and the upstream reach; the high-frequency map is brightest over the margins and the thinned downstream end. Each is a correct image of a different part of the channel, and the three together describe a geometry that no single map contains. Partyka, Gridley and Lopez set out this argument in 1999.
A component tuned to a thickness that is not present reports the thickest fill available. At 15 Hz, 1/(2f) predicts 33.3 ms, but the strongest response measured is at 27.6 ms. No fill on this channel is 33.3 ms thick, so the component responds most strongly to the thickest fill that exists, and the output gives no indication that this has happened. Raising the thalweg fill in the header brings the 15 Hz measurement toward 33.3 ms once fill of that thickness is present. The same effect returns in module 07, where it limits what the colors in a blend can mean.

4A section across the channel

Where each frequency puts its bright spot

Why this step is here. A high-frequency map shows two bright lines running side by side with a dark gap between them. What produces that pattern?

A profile across the channel shows the mechanism directly. The upper panel is the true fill thickness: greatest at the axis, thinning to zero at both margins. The lower panel is the amplitude of three frequency components measured along the same profile.

Each component reaches its maximum where the fill thickness equals the thickness that component responds to, so each maximum falls at a different distance from the axis. The high-frequency component reaches a maximum twice, once on each margin.

The top panel is the seismic itself: a section cut across the channel, with the true top and base of the fill marked on it. Depth in meters runs down the right-hand side against two-way time on the left, and the two scales are not proportional to each other.

A single channel produces two bright lines on a high-frequency map. On the midway profile the low-frequency component is strongest where the fill is 25.4 ms thick, at the axis. The high-frequency component is strongest where the fill is 9.4 ms thick, which occurs on both flanks. In map view the result is two bright lines separated by a dark gap. The gap lies over the thalweg and is dark because the fill there is too thick to respond at that frequency. A low-frequency map of the same interval is bright across the gap, which is the test that distinguishes this case from two separate channels.

5Three frequencies displayed together

The blend everyone has seen

Why this step is here. Spectral decomposition results are usually shown as a channel in red, green and blue. What quantity does each color represent?

Examining fifty iso-frequency maps individually is impractical, so the standard display combines three of them: a low frequency in red, a middle frequency in green, and a high frequency in blue. Fill that responds at the low frequency appears red, fill that responds at the high frequency appears blue, and fill that responds at two of them appears in the corresponding mixture.

The three maps are stacked rather than summed. Each layer is transparent where its frequency has little amplitude and opaque where it has a lot. Stacking means a layer can conceal one beneath it, and that the stacking order affects the result; module 07 examines both. For this module the point is that the color represents three maps at once, and that a single color bar cannot describe it.

The three maps that go into the blend are shown first, each on its own, each drawn in the color it will contribute. They are three iso-frequency maps of the kind step 3 produced, and each is bright over a different part of the channel.

Red channel low frequency

Green channel middle frequency

Blue channel high frequency

Displaying the three together paints each map over the one before it, in the order red, green, blue. A location bright on one map only takes that map's color. A location bright on two or three takes a mixture weighted toward whichever of them is painted last, because each layer is partly opaque where it is strong and covers part of what lies beneath. So the parts of the channel that respond at more than one frequency come back in the blues and greens rather than in yellow, cyan or white: the blue layer is on top here, and what it covers does not reach the screen at full strength. Module 07 puts the stacking order and the opacity on sliders, and describes the other convention in use, which writes the three maps straight into the display's red, green and blue channels and does produce white where all three are strong.

Each color in the blend names which of the three frequencies responded. Red marks fill that responds at the low frequency and not the other two, so it traces the thalweg and the upstream reach. Blue marks fill that responds only at the high frequency, along the margins and the thinned downstream end. Yellow and cyan mark fill in the overlap between two of them. The three panels above are the same three maps that produce those colors, and comparing a color on the blend with the panel it came from is the way to read one.
Noise degrades the blend more than it degrades the full-bandwidth map. On clean data the blend covers 87% of the channel against 73% for the amplitude map. At 10% noise that is 79% against 58%, at 20% it is 59% against 49%, and at 40% the order reverses: 21% against 35%. Each iso-frequency component uses a narrow slice of the band, so it carries a small share of the signal energy while the noise it competes with is spread across the whole spectrum. The full-bandwidth map sums across the band and holds up longer. The advantage a blend has on clean data is real and it is not unconditional.
The blend exceeds the brightness threshold over more of the channel than the amplitude map does. On the default channel the blend covers 87% against 73% for the full-bandwidth map. With no downstream thinning the difference is larger, 89% against 52%, because three responding thicknesses span more of the fill thicknesses present than one does. At 80% downstream thinning the difference falls to 83% against 70%: most of the fill is then thinner than even the high-frequency component responds to, so no choice of frequency reaches it. In that case the limit is the recorded band rather than the display. The blend does not give a calibrated thickness. Red indicates thicker fill than blue, and converting that to meters requires the arithmetic in module 04 and the wavelet correction in module 05.

Why it matters

What the module is for

This module covers one relationship: bed thickness against frequency. The rest of the set covers how to measure it properly.

  • Mapping a channel or a sand body. The outline from an amplitude map is a contour of one thickness rather than the edge of the sand. Several frequencies give several contours, and the shape of the body emerges from the set.
  • Separating thickness from lithology. A bright spot that moves as the frequency changes is a thickness effect. One that stays put across the band is more likely to be a contrast in rock properties.
  • Structural work as well as stratigraphic. A fault that thins a bed, a small flexure, and differential compaction over a buried body all change thickness laterally, and all appear on frequency maps that one amplitude map treats as a single uniform response.
  • Judging what a survey can do before the study is designed. The recorded band sets the thickness range available to work in, and step 1 is where to check it.

Three ways this goes wrong. The commonest is reading brightness on an iso-frequency map as sand quality or net-to-gross. It is an interference effect, and a thicker, poorer sand can be brighter than a thinner, better one. The second is comparing two frequencies without dealing with the wavelet first, since the data do not carry the same energy at 20 Hz and at 60 Hz; that comparison is partly a comparison of the source, which is module 05's subject. The third is assuming a channel bright at 40 Hz is 1/(2 × 40) thick. The relation holds for a clean two-reflector bed and degrades for anything with internal structure, which module 04 works through.

Selecting a frequency selects a bed thickness to be sensitive to. A single-frequency map is therefore sensitive to a single thickness.

Exercises

  1. Measure the thickness range a survey can resolve. Why: a survey resolves a particular range of bed thicknesses. A target either falls within that range or it does not. In step 1, set the wavelet peak to 20 Hz, then to 45 Hz, reading the thickness scale each time. Before dragging it: raising the frequency makes the wavelet shorter. Does that suit thicker beds or thinner ones?
    HintAt 20 Hz the usable band is about 10 to 33 Hz, suiting beds of 15.2 to 52.5 ms — 19 to 66 m of rock at 2500 m/s. At 45 Hz the band is 22 to 74 Hz and the range becomes 6.8 to 23.2 ms, or 8.5 to 29 m. The band slides and the thickness range slides with it. At the low setting the survey suits thick beds and is nearly blind to thin ones; at the high setting the reverse. If the target sits outside the range, the answer is different acquisition rather than more processing.
  2. Change the wavelet and watch the bright ribbon move. Why: two vintages of seismic over one field can locate the reservoir differently for this reason alone. In step 2, change only the wavelet's peak frequency and watch which part of the channel is brightest.
    HintThe channel does not move and the bright part does: brightest at 21.7 ms of fill with an 18 Hz wavelet, 13.0 ms at 30 Hz and 8.7 ms at 45 Hz, against a thalweg holding 25.4 ms throughout. At the low setting the axis is bright and the margins dark; at the high setting the margins light up and the axis dulls. The map is reporting where the fill thickness matches the wavelet, which is a fact about the survey as much as about the sand.
  3. Track the responding thickness across the band. Why: the most informative single frequency differs for every channel, which is why several are examined. In step 3, take the frequency slider from 8 Hz to 80 Hz slowly, watching the brightest-thickness readout and the area readout.
    HintThe brightest fill runs 27.6, 19.2, 12.8 and 8.7 ms as the frequency goes 15, 25, 40 and 60 Hz, against a prediction of 33.3, 20.0, 12.5 and 8.3. The lit zone walks from the thalweg outward as the frequency rises, and off the channel entirely once 1/(2f) falls below the thinnest fill present. The area readout peaks in the middle of the band rather than at either end, and that peak is the single frequency showing the most of this particular channel.
  4. Account for a channel that appears as two features. Why: a dark axis between two bright lines is a thickness effect, and it has been interpreted as levees and as twin channels. In step 4, set the frequency high and look at the cross-channel profile, then compare with a low frequency.
    HintOn the midway profile the low component peaks on 25.4 ms of fill and the high one on 9.4 ms. The high component therefore peaks twice, once on each margin where the fill has thinned into its tuning range, and is quiet over the thalweg where the fill is too thick for it. In map view that reads as two parallel bright lines with a dark gap, and the gap is the thickest part of the channel. The check is a low-frequency map of the same interval, where the gap fills in.
  5. Measure how much the blend adds, and at what point it stops adding. Why: blending helps when the target's thickness range overlaps the recorded band, and cannot help when it does not. In step 5, compare the two area readouts at 0%, 50% and 80% downstream thinning.
    HintThe blend lights 87% of the channel against 73% for the amplitude map on the default settings, and 89% against 52% with no downstream thinning. Three tuning thicknesses cover more of the fill thicknesses present than one does. Take the thinning to 80% and the gap narrows to 83% against 70%, because by then most of the fill is thinner than even the high component can tune. No display choice recovers a target the survey did not record.

If you remember nothing else

  • Seismic occupies a narrow band, and each frequency in it responds most strongly to one bed thickness. A frequency f is tuned to a bed 1/(2f) thick, so the recorded band sets the thickness range a survey can work in.
  • A full-bandwidth amplitude map contours one bed thickness rather than outlining a geological body. The bright part of a channel is where the fill is closest to the wavelet's tuning thickness.
  • Different frequencies image different parts of the same feature. Low frequencies respond to thick fill and high frequencies to thin fill. Both maps are correct.
  • A high-frequency map can show one channel as two bright lines with a dark axis, because the fill at the thalweg is too thick to respond at that frequency.
  • Blending three frequencies covers more of a body than any single map: 87% against 73% on this channel, provided the body's thickness range overlaps the recorded band.
  • Brightness on a frequency map measures interference rather than rock quality. Converting it to a thickness requires the arithmetic in module 04 and the wavelet correction in module 05.

Words introduced here

acoustic impedance
Density times velocity. A contrast in it across a boundary is what produces a reflection.
reflection coefficient
The fraction of the arriving wave amplitude that returns from a boundary. Positive when impedance increases downward, negative when it decreases.
two-way time
The time for a wave to travel down to a boundary and back. Seismic sections are plotted in it, so a 24 ms bed at 2500 m/s is about 30 m of rock.
thalweg
The deepest line along a channel; here, the axis where the fill is thickest.
amplitude spectrum
How much of each frequency a wavelet or a trace contains, plotted against frequency. Module 01 builds one from scratch.
peak frequency
The frequency at which the amplitude spectrum is highest.
magnitude
How much energy sits at one frequency at one place in the trace. The maps on this page are maps of magnitude, one frequency at a time.
tuning
The interference between the top and base reflections of a bed, once they are close enough in time to overlap. Module 04 works through it.
bandwidth
The width of the spectrum. Here, the range over which the wavelet carries at least half its peak amplitude. Module 01 measures it.
tuning thickness
The bed thickness a given frequency responds to most strongly, 1/(2f) in two-way time. Module 04 derives it and distinguishes it from a second quantity with the same name.
iso-frequency map
A map made from one narrow frequency component of the data rather than from the full bandwidth.
spectral component, or voice
The part of the data at one narrow frequency. Module 03 opens one up.
RGB blend
Three iso-frequency maps displayed together as red, green and blue. Module 07 takes one apart.

Method

What is computed, and what is left out

The channel is a sinuous axis across a 140 by 120 grid, with fill thickness falling from the thalweg to the margins on a cosine profile and thinning downstream by the fraction set on the slider. At every grid point the fill is a pair of reflection coefficients, +0.1 at the top and −0.1 at the base, separated by the local thickness, convolved with a zero-phase Ricker. Reflection times are analytic rather than rounded to the sample grid.

The three channel presets set the thalweg fill, width and downstream thinning sliders and nothing else. They are three shapes worth comparing rather than three named depositional settings.

The iso-frequency maps are computed, not drawn. At every grid point the synthetic trace is windowed over the channel interval and transformed directly at the requested frequency, and the magnitude of that one complex number becomes the map value. A full transform at every point and every frequency would be sixteen thousand transforms per redraw; taking one frequency at a time is the same arithmetic with the unused frequencies left out.

The area readouts count grid points where the map exceeds 70% of its own maximum and the true fill exceeds 2 ms, as a percentage of all points where fill exists. The same threshold and denominator are used for the blend and for the full-bandwidth map, so the two numbers can be compared with each other. They should not be compared with anything outside this module.

Simplifications, stated plainly. There is no noise, no dip, no compaction and no lateral velocity variation, so the channel sits flat and the extraction window is the same everywhere. The fill has a single uniform impedance, which makes the model a clean two-reflector bed at every point; real channel fill is layered, and internal reflections put structure in the spectrum that this model cannot produce. The wavelet is zero phase and the same everywhere. No spectral balancing is applied, so the energy available at 20 Hz and at 60 Hz is not the same, and these maps carry that bias deliberately — removing it is module 05.

Where this departs from production software. A real workflow decomposes a whole volume, then extracts along an interpreted horizon or within a stratal slab, and the extraction geometry is usually what decides whether the result is any good. Here the interval is known exactly because the model made it. Real blends are also usually built from balanced volumes and often use hue and lightness rather than three color channels, which module 07 covers.
